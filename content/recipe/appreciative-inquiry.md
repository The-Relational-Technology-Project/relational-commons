# Appreciative Inquiry

> Stop diagnosing what's broken. Ask the group what's already working and build from there.

## What it is

A community development methodology developed by David Cooperrider and Diana Whitney that shifts conversation away from problem-finding and toward strength-finding. Groups gather stories of moments when the community was at its best, identify the conditions that made those moments possible, and design forward from there.

The framework moves through four phases (the "4-D Cycle"): **Discover** what's working. **Dream** what could be. **Design** the propositions and structures that move toward it. **Destiny** the commitments and actions that make it real.

## Why it works

Most community processes start by asking "what's wrong here." That framing trains people to see deficits, blame, and lack. It also tends to disempower the people closest to the situation, who become "problems to solve" rather than authors of solutions.

AI inverts the question. People become experts on the times their community was at its best. The stories they tell carry the specific conditions, relationships, and choices that produced those moments. The forward design grows out of evidence of what's possible because it actually happened.

It's not naïve. AI doesn't deny problems. It just sequences the conversation differently: strengths first, then aspirations, then design.

## What you need

- **Time:** 3 to 12 months for a full cycle. Often a 2 to 4 day "summit" anchors the intensive work.
- **People:** 30+ community members representing diverse perspectives. Trained interviewers.
- **Space:** Interview spaces (one-on-one or small group). A summit venue with breakout areas.
- **Materials:** Interview guides, recording equipment, flip charts, story synthesis tools.
- **Skills:** Appreciative listening, strength-spotting, facilitation of visioning, asset mapping.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. Builds momentum through story collection.

## How to host it

### Phase 1: Discover (4 to 8 weeks)

1. **Craft the appreciative interview question.** The question should prompt stories of peak moments. Examples: *Tell me about a time when this neighborhood felt most alive. Describe a moment when you knew this community could do anything. When have you seen our institutions at their best?*
2. **Train interviewers.** Often community members themselves. They learn to listen for the texture of the story, the specific conditions, the relationships and choices that mattered.
3. **Conduct interviews.** Aim for 30 to 100 interviews depending on community size. Each interview is 30 to 60 minutes. Record or take detailed notes.
4. **Analyze the stories.** Identify themes, repeated conditions, unique factors (leadership patterns, relationships, cultural practices) that made the peak moments possible.

### Phase 2: Dream (a session or two)

5. **Gather the broader community.** A summit format works well. People share the stories they heard.
6. **Invite the room to imagine.** "If our community amplified what was happening in these stories, what would it look like in five years?" Visual, narrative, sometimes embodied.

### Phase 3: Design (a session or two)

7. **Create provocative propositions.** Statements describing the community as if the dream were already true. Bold, specific, written in present tense. Example: *"In our neighborhood, every newcomer is greeted within their first week and has at least three named connections by the end of their first month."*
8. **Identify the structures that make each proposition real.** Practices, programs, agreements, infrastructure, decision-making processes.

### Phase 4: Destiny (ongoing)

9. **Commit to specific actions.** Who is doing what, by when. Tied to the propositions.
10. **Implement and reflect.** What's working? What needs adjustment? AI sees implementation as another cycle of inquiry, not an end point.

## Variations

**Mini-AI for a specific question.** A weekend version focused on one specific community challenge. Interview phase compressed; summit lasts a day.

**AI inside an organization.** Used widely in nonprofits, schools, and faith communities for strategic planning. Often produces longer-lasting change than traditional SWOT-style strategy work.

**AI as orientation.** A new group or coalition uses the appreciative interview as the first conversation. Sets a culture from the start.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The methodology emerged from David Cooperrider and Diana Whitney at Case Western Reserve University. The Center for Appreciative Inquiry has documented applications across community development organizations, municipal governments, schools, faith communities, and rural development initiatives.

In neighborhood-scale work, AI has been used by communities recovering from disasters, neighborhoods undergoing gentrification, schools rebuilding trust with families, and faith communities renewing their sense of purpose.
