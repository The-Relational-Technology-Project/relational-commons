# Repair Cafe

> A free community event where people fix broken things together with skilled volunteers. Lamps, clothes, bikes, toasters.

## What it is

A few-hour event where neighbors bring broken items and work alongside volunteer "fixers" to repair them rather than throw them out. Clothes, electronics, bicycles, furniture, ceramics, small appliances. People come in expecting to learn something. They leave with a working thing and a few new neighbors.

Repair Cafes are a quiet movement. More than 2,000 of them run regularly in 37 countries.

## Why it works

Three things at once: less waste, less money spent, more relationships. The act of fixing something with another person is intimate in a low-key way. There's a problem in front of you both. You solve it together. By the end you know each other's names and you have a working lamp. That dynamic, repeated across a room of twenty pairs, produces a particular kind of community feeling that's hard to manufacture any other way.

## What you need

- **Time:** 4 to 6 weeks of planning. A full day for the event.
- **People:** 5 to 10 volunteer fixers. 2 to 3 organizers. 20 to 50 participants per event.
- **Space:** A community venue with tables, chairs, good lighting, and electrical outlets. A church hall, library, school cafeteria, or community center.
- **Materials:** Basic shared tools, solder, sewing supplies, glue. Fixers bring specialized tools. Tablecloths to protect surfaces. Refreshments.
- **Skills:** Repair knowledge across categories. Light coordination. Outreach.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. Hands-on but social.

## How to host it

1. **Request the starter kit from [repaircafe.org](https://www.repaircafe.org/en/).** It includes templates, intake forms, liability language, and signage. Use it. The Foundation makes this freely available.
2. **Recruit fixers.** Reach out to local makers, hobbyists, retired engineers, seamstresses, bike mechanics, electronics tinkerers. Most communities have more skilled fixers than people realize. Three to five categories of repair is plenty: electrical, textiles, bikes, small appliances, ceramics.
3. **Find a venue.** Library meeting rooms, faith spaces, community centers, school cafeterias. Free or low-cost. Make sure there's enough power, light, and table space.
4. **Set up intake.** Each participant fills out a short intake card describing the item and the problem. This helps fixers triage and protects both parties around liability.
5. **Promote 3 to 4 weeks out.** Flyers, neighborhood mailing lists, Nextdoor, [Front Porch Forum](../reference/front-porch-forum.md). List the categories of repair you'll have available. People won't bring things if they don't know it's possible.
6. **Day of: set up zones.** One area per repair category. Refreshments away from electronics. A welcome table for intake. A "next steps" sign for items that can't be fixed today.
7. **Pace the event.** 3 to 4 hours is right. Longer drains fixers. Encourage participants to stay for the whole repair, not just drop off.
8. **Close warmly.** Group photo of what got fixed. Thanks to fixers. Date for the next one.

## Variations

**Monthly Cafe.** Smaller, more regular. Lower bar to attend. Higher relationship density over time.

**Themed Cafe.** Bike repair only. Mending only. E-waste only. Sometimes the focused version draws people who'd be intimidated by the general one.

**Library partnership.** Many libraries now host monthly Repair Cafes. They have the space, the audience, and the institutional staying power. Pitch this to your local library if you don't have a venue.

**Pop-up at events.** A Repair Cafe table at a [block party](../reference/block-party.md), farmers market, or festival.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Martine Postma**, a Dutch journalist, launched the first Repair Cafe in Amsterdam on October 18, 2009, at Fijnhout Theater. She'd noticed how many usable items neighbors threw away because they couldn't fix them. The Repair Cafe Foundation was established in March 2010.

Today over 2,000 Repair Cafes operate in 37 countries. The Foundation provides starter kits, ongoing support, and a network of organizers in the open. The model is a near-perfect example of what this book is about: a real practice, kept open by its founders, spread by neighbors, with no platform extraction in between.
