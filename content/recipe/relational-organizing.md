# Relational Organizing

> Organizing through existing relationships instead of cold outreach. The work moves through trusted networks, person by person.

## What it is

A community organizing approach where the unit of work is the one-on-one conversation, and the unit of growth is the relationship that already exists. Organizers identify people in a community who have natural credibility and strong ties. Those connectors reach into their networks. The invitation to participate, vote, donate, or act arrives through someone the receiver already trusts.

The method draws from many traditions: labor organizing, faith-based organizing (Industrial Areas Foundation, PICO/Faith in Action), community organizing in the Saul Alinsky lineage, civil rights movement organizing. It has been re-popularized recently by [Southerners on New Ground](https://southernersonnewground.org/) and other contemporary movements.

## Why it works

Cold outreach treats people as numbers. Relational organizing treats people as relationships. The first approach scales by volume. The second scales by trust. When someone you already know and respect asks you to do something, the request crosses the gap that a stranger's call never could.

The one-on-one conversation is the basic unit. Done well, it is a 30- to 60-minute mutual inquiry about what each person cares about, what they're working on, and what they might do together. The relationship itself is the product. The action that comes out of it is the second-order effect.

## What you need

- **Time:** Ongoing. Relationship-building runs in months and years. Each one-on-one is 30 to 60 minutes.
- **People:** Key connectors with strong existing relationships. Trained organizers or coordinators. The wider community network.
- **Space:** Informal spaces: coffee shops, homes, workplaces, parks, community centers.
- **Materials:** A contact tracking system (a simple spreadsheet or a CRM). Conversation guides if useful. Background materials on issues.
- **Skills:** Active listening, relationship-building, one-on-one facilitation, knowledge of the local context.
- **Energy level:** Medium. Sustained over a long time.

## How to host it

1. **Identify the connectors.** Who in your community already has trust capital? Pastors, hairdressers, bartenders, teachers, longtime residents, faith leaders, restaurant owners, recovery group leaders. Not necessarily the people with the loudest voices; the people other people listen to.
2. **Have one-on-ones with each of them.** Not a pitch. A real conversation. *What are you working on? What matters to you about this neighborhood? Who else should I be talking to?* Listen for hours, not minutes. Don't rush.
3. **The good one-on-one has a structure.** 30 to 60 minutes. About half listening to them, about half about the work or the cause. Always ends with: *Who else should I be talking to? Can you make that introduction?*
4. **Map the network.** As one-on-ones produce names, draw the network. Who knows whom. Who has influence in which subgroup. Where the gaps are.
5. **Invite connectors into shared work.** This is where it tips from "I'm getting to know people" to "we're doing something together." A small initial commitment: come to a meeting, bring two neighbors, contribute one thing.
6. **Use the network for asks, not just contacts.** When the campaign or project needs people to show up, vote, write, or give, the ask moves through the network. Not a mass email. A real ask from one trusted person to another.
7. **Hold meetings of connectors.** The connectors themselves benefit from knowing each other. Quarterly gatherings, retreats, dinners. Their relationships with each other strengthen the network as a whole.
8. **Sustain it over years.** This is the part that organizations under-invest in. Relational organizing is not a quarterly tactic. It is a way of working that compounds over decades.

## Variations

**Listening campaigns.** A focused round of one-on-ones (often 100 to 1,000) to surface what a community actually cares about, before deciding what to organize around. Industrial Areas Foundation does this well.

**Relational voter contact.** Voter turnout work done through the network: friends texting friends, neighbors knocking on neighbors. Higher conversion than cold contact.

**Faith-based organizing.** Relational organizing rooted in congregations. PICO/Faith in Action, IAF, and Gamaliel are the main networks.

**Healthcare community organizing.** Hospitals and health systems increasingly use relational organizing to build community partnerships. The model adapts.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The lineage is deep. **Ella Baker's** organizing work in the civil rights movement was profoundly relational, focused on building local leaders rather than charismatic figureheads. **Saul Alinsky's** Industrial Areas Foundation institutionalized the one-on-one as a core organizing practice. **PICO (now Faith in Action)** built faith-based federations that run on the one-on-one. **Southerners on New Ground** organize across race, class, gender, and sexuality through deep relational practice.

Contemporary campaigns on labor, immigration, climate, housing, and electoral work have all used relational organizing. The pattern is consistent: campaigns built on real relationships outlast campaigns built on lists.
