# Block Stewards

> Be the neighbor who keeps the block connected. Quietly. Over years.

## What it is

A Block Steward is a committed neighbor who serves as a connector for their block: getting to know everyone, noticing who has what skills or needs, making introductions, and occasionally pulling people together. The role is informal. It is also one of the most powerful pieces of community infrastructure that exists.

A block with even one steward is a different block than one without.

## Why it works

Most blocks have people who would help each other if they knew each other. The information just doesn't move. A steward is the moving piece. They are the person who knows that the woman in #4 has a ladder, the family in #7 has a new baby, the older man in #12 just lost his wife, and the kids in #14 are looking for someone to walk them home from school.

This is not surveillance. It is knowing. The difference is consent and care. Stewards build the kind of knowing that makes mutual aid possible without anyone having to set up a mutual aid pod first.

## What you need

- **Time:** 5 to 10 hours a week, but flexible and self-determined. Some weeks more, some weeks almost none.
- **People:** Ideally 1 to 2 stewards per block. Solo works. Pairs work better.
- **Space:** The block itself. Your home, your porch, neighbors' homes, the sidewalk.
- **Materials:** A way to keep track of names and details. A notebook is fine. A simple list works. Avoid spreadsheets that feel like databases.
- **Skills:** Listening. Memory for names. Comfort starting conversations. Restraint from pushing.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. It is a relational practice, not a project. Energy comes from how much you enjoy people.

## How to host it

1. **Decide that you're doing this.** No one will ask you to be a steward. The role is self-claimed. You don't need to tell anyone you're doing it. You just start.
2. **Make a list of every household on your block.** Address by address. You'll fill in names as you learn them.
3. **Walk the block on purpose, regularly.** Same loop, same route. People will start to recognize you. Wave. Stop when invited.
4. **Introduce yourself to one new household a week.** Not all at once. Slow is sustainable. Use a small reason: a flyer for a block thing, a question about the neighborhood, an offer of something from your garden.
5. **Listen for what people are working on, struggling with, or proud of.** This is the steward's real skill. Not gathering data, but remembering what matters to people.
6. **Make introductions.** When you learn that the new family in #9 is also vegan and so is the couple in #2, mention it. When the older man in #12 says he misses his garden and you know the family in #5 has yard space, mention it. Most stewardship is moving information between people who would have hit it off if only they'd known.
7. **Host one small thing a few times a year.** A porch coffee morning. A holiday card exchange. A summer block party. The point of the small things is not the events. It's giving people who know you a low-pressure way to meet each other.
8. **Don't become the bottleneck.** A good steward grows other stewards. If you find a neighbor who's energized by this work, fold them in. The block needs many connectors, not one super-connector.

## Variations

**Apartment building version.** Stewards for floors or wings. Same role, smaller scope.

**Pair version.** Two stewards per block, working together. Reduces burnout. Doubles the relationship network.

**Steward circle version.** Stewards from several blocks meet monthly to share what they're seeing and learning. Useful in larger neighborhoods.

**With technology.** Some stewards use small tools (a contact list, a group text, a [Front Porch Forum](../reference/front-porch-forum.md) board). Use what helps. Avoid anything that becomes the relationship instead of supporting it.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Common Agency**, where this practice is being formalized, has trained Block Stewards across multiple neighborhoods. **Deborah Tien** leads this work. Common Agency's stewards have organized plant exchanges, multilingual children's book hours, garden coordinations, and informal coffee gatherings.

**Lori**, a Network Steward in Eastcore, organized a plant exchange after discovering many neighbors had plants to share. **Vika** in Metuchen saw a gap in multilingual children's services and created a quiet solution: she glued translations into children's books and organized multilingual story time where neighbors read in their own languages.

Both examples are stewards doing what stewards do: noticing what neighbors already have, then helping them realize they could do something with it together.
