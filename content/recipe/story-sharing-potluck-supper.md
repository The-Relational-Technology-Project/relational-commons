# Story-Sharing Potluck Supper

> A shared meal with a story prompt. Goes deeper than small talk on purpose.

## What it is

A communal meal where everyone brings a dish and also brings a two-to-three-minute story shaped by a prompt. The prompt is the difference. It's what moves the table from "nice to meet you" to "I will remember you."

A good story supper makes 15 strangers feel like 15 people who know something real about each other within three hours.

## Why it works

Most adult conversation defaults to surface. A prompt that is personal but not partisan gives people permission to go one layer down. The food gives them something to do with their hands while they listen. The structure makes it safe to actually speak.

This isn't therapy. It's the opposite of therapy. It's everyday narrative skill, practiced in the company of neighbors.

## What you need

- **Time:** 4 to 6 weeks planning. About 3 hours for the event.
- **People:** 2 to 3 organizers. 15 to 30 attendees works best. Smaller is more intimate, larger needs more structure.
- **Space:** A warm room. A community center, a church basement, a backyard, a large dining room. Tables that can be arranged so everyone can see everyone.
- **Materials:** Tables, chairs, tablecloths, place settings. Notebooks if you want to compile recipes or stories afterward. Flowers if you want them.
- **Skills:** Light facilitation. Enough comfort to hold the room when one story runs long or another stays quiet.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. The emotional work of creating safety is the most demanding part.

## How to host it

1. **Pick the prompt first.** This is the most important decision. Make it personal but not partisan. Examples that work: *Share a food from your childhood and what it meant to you. Tell us about a meal that brought someone unexpected into your life. Share a place you've left and what you carry from it.* Avoid prompts that ask about politics, religion, or "what's wrong with the world."
2. **Set a date and invite.** Send the invitation 3 to 4 weeks out. Tell people up front that they will share a 2-to-3 minute story responding to the prompt. This is the most important sentence in your invitation. It is also the one that scares people. Reassure them: it's a story, not a speech.
3. **Plan the meal.** Potluck works. Or pick a theme that fits the prompt (childhood foods, foods from where people are from). Don't over-engineer. People will bring more than enough.
4. **Arrange the room.** One long table if possible. If you have to do multiple tables, build in a moment when stories are shared across them.
5. **Open warmly.** Welcome people. Explain the rhythm. Read the prompt aloud. Set expectations: 2 to 3 minutes each, no questions interrupting, applause is welcome.
6. **Do a first round with volunteers.** Two or three people who agreed in advance to go first. They warm up the room.
7. **Eat.** Stories continue informally as people serve themselves. Encourage table conversation about what they've heard.
8. **Do a closing round before dessert.** Anyone who hasn't spoken yet who wants to. A practiced storyteller can perform something prepared.
9. **Collect.** Pass around a notebook for recipes, lines, or favorite moments. If you want, compile a small cookbook afterward and send it to everyone who came. This is the artifact that keeps the supper alive in people's homes.

## Variations

**Smaller version.** 6 to 10 people, dining room, one shared meal cooked together. Closer to a dinner party. Less ceremony, deeper conversation.

**Public version.** 50 to 100 people, library or community center. Needs more structure and stronger facilitation. See [The Longest Table](../recipe/the-longest-table.md) for the much larger format.

**Theme variations.** Migration stories. Place stories. Hands stories (something your hands have learned to do). Loss stories (in a healing-focused setting, see [Dinner Party Grief](../recipe/dinner-party-grief.md)). Letter-to-younger-self stories.

**Cross-generational version.** Pair an elder with a teenager at each seat. Prompt is shaped to invite mentorship and curiosity.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Community Plate**, based in Maine, has facilitated story-sharing suppers across the state, from Lewiston to Lubec. NPR featured their work in a 2025 story about fighting loneliness through community suppers.

At one coastal Maine supper, an elderly immigrant shared how her mother's recipe was her anchor when arriving in America. A teenager heard this and realized her grandmother made the same dish. After the supper, the grandmother began teaching the teenager her family cooking tradition, something that hadn't happened in years. The supper didn't "solve" isolation. It made one new intergenerational relationship possible.
