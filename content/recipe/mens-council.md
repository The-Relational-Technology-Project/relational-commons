# Men's Council

> Eight to twelve men, sitting in a circle, taking turns telling the truth. No fixing, no advice, no rush.

## What it is

A facilitated listening circle where men gather regularly to speak and listen with vulnerability and integrity. The format is borrowed from older circle traditions, including Indigenous council practices. The work of the council is mutual recognition: men hearing each other without trying to solve, perform, or compete.

Council is one of the most reliable forms of emotional infrastructure for men, who in most modern contexts lack a structured place to be honest with each other about their lives.

## Why it works

Men in many cultures are trained to relate through doing, performing, and problem-solving. Council interrupts that pattern. The talking piece, the turn-taking, the deep listening protocol create a space where the usual moves are unavailable. What emerges is what doesn't usually get said out loud: about marriage, fathering, work, grief, purpose, fear, masculinity itself.

Over months of regular meetings, the trust deepens until council becomes the place where the harder, slower personal work can actually happen.

## What you need

- **Time:** 90 minutes to 2 hours per session, monthly or biweekly, recurring indefinitely.
- **People:** 8 to 12 men per circle. A trained facilitator is essential.
- **Space:** A comfortable room with privacy and chairs in a circle. No tables in the middle.
- **Materials:** A talking piece (optional). Posted ground rules. Refreshments. Comfortable seating.
- **Skills:** Deep listening, non-judgment, comfort with silence, basic facilitation.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. Emotionally engaged but not physically demanding.

## How to host it

1. **Get trained.** This is the one recipe in this book where outside training matters. [Journeymen](https://www.journeymen.us/mens-council) trains facilitators in council practice. There are other paths (ManKind Project, Sacred Sons, local circle traditions). Find one. Apprentice with an experienced facilitator before opening your own circle.
2. **Recruit 8 to 12 men.** Personal invitations. People you trust to follow through on a multi-month commitment.
3. **Hold an orientation meeting.** Explain the format. Set expectations. Establish confidentiality. Have everyone speak briefly about what brought them.
4. **Set a regular meeting time.** Same day each month or every two weeks. Predictability builds the container.
5. **Run the format.** A typical session:
   - Opening grounding (breath, silence, intention).
   - Check-in round: each man speaks briefly about how he arrives.
   - Theme or open council: men speak as called, using the talking piece. No interruption, no fixing, no advice.
   - Listening pauses between speakers.
   - Closing round.
6. **Protect the rules.** Confidentiality is absolute. No advice unless requested. No interrupting. No therapizing. The facilitator gently brings people back to the form.
7. **Allow the circle to deepen.** First few months are introductions. Months 3 to 12 are where the real work begins. Years are where the relationships become foundational.

## Variations

**Boys' Council.** Adapted for adolescent boys. [Journeymen](https://www.journeymen.us/) and others run formal programs. Schools have adopted the model.

**Women's Council.** Same form, different ground.

**Mixed council.** Some traditions run mixed-gender or non-gendered councils. Different dynamic, same protocol.

**Theme council.** A council formed around a shared identity or experience: fathers, recovery, queer men, men of color, men leaving organized religion.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Journeymen** is a 501(c)(3) based on Vashon Island, Washington, founded on principles of mentorship and social-emotional learning. Their Men's Council program has facilitated groups across the Pacific Northwest and beyond. They also run Boys' Council and the CIRCLES program for schools.

Participants in long-running councils report deeper emotional expression, stronger connection to themselves and others, and healthier ways of relating to other men in their lives. Many councils stay together for years, becoming a primary friendship structure for their members.
