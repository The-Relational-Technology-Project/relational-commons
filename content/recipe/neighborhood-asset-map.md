# The Asset Map (Not the Needs Map)

> Asset-based community development's core practice: before listing what your neighborhood lacks, inventory what it has — residents' gifts, associations, institutions, places, exchange, and stories — and connect them.

*The need:* Neighborhoods described only by their deficits start believing the description.

John Kretzmann and John McKnight's 'Building Communities from the Inside Out' (1993) opens with two maps of the same place. The **needs map** — poverty rates, crime stats, dropout numbers — is 'part of the truth' mistaken for the whole truth; taken as the map, it teaches residents 'to see themselves as people with special needs that can only be met by outsiders,' routes money to service providers instead of residents, and forces leaders to win funding by denigrating their neighbors. The **assets map** starts from what is present: it is asset-based, internally focused, and relationship-driven.

What goes on the assets map (the tradition's six categories): the **gifts of residents** (skills, knowledge, passions); **associations** (block clubs, choirs, leagues, the domino table — 'an association is the collective word for citizen'); **institutions** (schools, libraries, parks, churches, businesses); **physical places**; **exchange** (barter, timebanks, buying clubs, money); and **stories** — 'treasure maps that help us discover the hidden bounty.'

How a neighborhood actually does it, per McKnight and Russell's sequence: **discover** the neighborhood's natural connectors → **welcome** everyone, especially people at the margins, through learning conversations and listening campaigns → **portray** what you've heard (this is the map — a byproduct of conversations, never a data-scrape) → **share** through skill swaps, repair cafés, seed exchanges → **celebrate** → **vision** together.

Two cautions from the source itself: an asset map does not mean the neighborhood needs nothing from outside — internal assets are 'absolutely necessary but usually not sufficient'; and ABCD is meant to complement and often precede organizing, not substitute for it. The map hands the organizing arc its answer to question three — and a gift list waiting for question four.

## Details

- **field tips:** inventory associations, not just individuals — McKnight's checklist runs from block clubs to stamp collectors, the most powerful communities identify the gifts of people at the margins, never let the map substitute for the conversations
- **ingredients:** a few connectors, learning conversations block by block, an association inventory, somewhere shared to portray it all
- **source note:** Needs-map/assets-map argument and quotes from the Green Book introduction (reprintable with attribution); six assets and discover→vision sequence from 'The Four Essential Elements of an ABCD Process' (CC BY-NC 4.0).
- **how it works:** Discover the natural connectors and convene them., Welcome: run gift conversations door by door, margins first., Portray: compile a shared, evolving picture — the map is the byproduct, the relationships are the product., Share: skill swaps, repair cafés, seed exchanges — on-ramps where gifts meet., Celebrate, then vision together — and only then bridge to outside resources.
- **min resources:** Free. Clipboards.
- **practice kind:** recipe
- **success signals:** neighbors are introduced to each other's gifts, not just listed, an association does something beyond its founding purpose, outside agencies respond to a community agenda instead of setting one
- **asset categories:** residents' gifts, associations, institutions, physical places, exchange, stories
