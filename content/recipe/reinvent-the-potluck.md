# Reinvent the Potluck

> A potluck with a purpose. The food is the welcome; the structure is the work.

## What it is

A potluck that adds light facilitation. People still bring dishes. People still eat together. But the gathering is shaped around a question, a decision, a skill-share, or a relationship-building practice. The meal anchors the social warmth. The facilitation moves the room from socializing to actually doing something together.

The term comes from a [Shareable](https://www.shareable.net/how-to/how-to-reinvent-the-potluck/) framing of an older practice. Communities have done this forever; the recipe just names it.

## Why it works

A meeting is intimidating. A potluck is not. Most neighbors will come to a potluck who wouldn't come to a "community meeting." When you add a clear, light frame to the potluck, you get the participation of the potluck and the productivity of the meeting in the same room.

The eating-together is doing real work. People relax. They tell stories. They notice each other. By the time the structured part starts, the room is warmer than any meeting agenda could engineer.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 3 hours for the event. 2 to 3 weeks of light planning.
- **People:** A host or small organizing crew. 8 to 30 attendees works best.
- **Space:** A home, community room, backyard, or park.
- **Materials:** Whatever the dish-sharing logistics require. Optional: a printed prompt, a flip chart, a talking piece.
- **Skills:** Light facilitation. Comfort framing a question and bringing the room to it.
- **Energy level:** Moderate.

## How to host it

1. **Decide the purpose.** A potluck without a purpose is just a potluck. (That's fine too; see [Block Party](../reference/block-party.md) and [Story-Sharing Potluck](../recipe/story-sharing-potluck-supper.md).) But "reinventing" means you have a specific intention. Examples:
   - Decide on a project the group will take on together.
   - Share skills around a theme (canning, mending, civic engagement).
   - Welcome new neighbors and make introductions across the group.
   - Plan for an upcoming neighborhood event.
   - Process something that happened in the neighborhood (a loss, a conflict, a change).
2. **Invite with the purpose named.** Don't surprise people with structure. The invitation should say: *We'll eat from 6 to 7. From 7 to 8 we'll have a short structured conversation about [purpose]. Bring a dish and your thoughts.*
3. **Coordinate dishes.** A simple shared sheet to prevent four pasta dishes. Or just trust your neighbors.
4. **Welcome and eat first.** Don't rush the meal. The food has to do its work before the structure does.
5. **Open the structured time.** A clear transition: "Let's gather around. I want to take 30 to 60 minutes to do the thing we came together for."
6. **Use a method that fits.** For decisions: dot voting, [community agreements](../recipe/community-agreements.md). For relationship: a story round, a [reciprocity game](../recipe/reciprocity-game.md). For skill share: rotating short demos. For shared problem-solving: a small [World Cafe](../recipe/world-cafe.md).
7. **Close with commitments or appreciations.** Each person, briefly: what they're taking, what they're committing to, or what they appreciate from the night.
8. **Linger.** The structured time should not consume the whole evening. Leave space for dessert, more conversation, the relationships that the structured time made possible.

## Variations

**Repeating potluck.** A monthly version with a different purpose each time. Builds rhythm and trust over months.

**Single-issue potluck.** Organized around one specific local issue: traffic on the street, a school decision, a budget proposal. The food creates room for the issue to be discussed without performance.

**Skill potluck.** Each person brings a dish and a skill. The skill share happens between the meal and dessert. Lighter form of a [skillshare](../recipe/skillshare.md).

**Outdoor potluck.** A park, a public plaza. Less control over weather, more visibility to passersby. Strong for [placemaking](../recipe/placemaking.md) work.

## Stories from neighborhoods

Community organizers have used potlucks as organizing tools for decades. Faith communities, labor organizers, civil rights movements, mutual aid pods, neighborhood associations. The reframing as a "reinvented" practice in the Shareable toolkit names a discipline that has run quietly for a long time.

Online coordination platforms like [Meal Train](https://www.mealtrain.com/), [Perfect Potluck](https://www.perfectpotluck.com/), and shared spreadsheets reduce the friction of coordinating dishes across larger groups.
