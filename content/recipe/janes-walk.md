# Jane's Walk

> A free walking tour of your neighborhood, led by someone who lives there. No experts required.

## What it is

A citizen-led walking conversation through a neighborhood. The walker who leads it isn't a tour guide or an urban planner. They're someone who lives there and has stories to tell. Walks are free, open to anyone, and typically last an hour or two.

Jane's Walks are named for Jane Jacobs, the urbanist who insisted that cities are best understood at walking pace. Since 2006 they've happened in over 500 cities globally, especially in early May around her birthday.

## Why it works

Walking shifts something. You meet your neighbors at a different angle. The street that you pass at 30 miles an hour every day reveals itself differently at 3 miles an hour. Walking together also dissolves the speaker-audience hierarchy that other formats build in. Everyone is on the same pavement.

A neighborhood that gets walked is a neighborhood that gets known. A neighborhood that gets known is one where people start to feel ownership.

## What you need

- **Time:** 6 weeks of light planning. 1 to 2 hours for the walk itself.
- **People:** A walk leader. A handful of curious neighbors. No upper limit; large groups can split.
- **Space:** Whatever you want to show. Streets, parks, alleys, plazas. Pick a route you can lead confidently.
- **Materials:** A simple map (drawn or printed). Optional: printouts of photos, historical documents, or stories. A clipboard or notebook for notes.
- **Skills:** Local knowledge. Storytelling. Comfortable speaking outdoors. Basic route planning.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. The walking itself is light. The hosting is real but not heavy.

## How to host it

1. **Pick your route.** Two to three miles is plenty for two hours. Fewer if you have a lot to show. Pick somewhere with stories. Avoid heavy traffic and steep grades unless you're prepared to handle accessibility.
2. **Pick your stops.** Five to ten places where you'll pause and tell a story. Each story is two to five minutes. Examples: where the old grocery store used to be, the corner where someone organized a protest in 1972, the tree planted when a kid was born, the contested intersection.
3. **Walk the route yourself first.** Time it. Note hazards. Note seating for breaks.
4. **Invite people.** A simple flyer, a [Front Porch Forum](../reference/front-porch-forum.md) post, a few words at a neighborhood meeting. Tell people it's free, no signup, all ages, all paces. List start time, start location, expected length.
5. **Start on time.** Welcome people. Set expectations: walking pace, ask questions whenever, stick together at intersections. Two minutes of orientation is enough.
6. **At each stop, tell the story.** Be specific. Name people, dates, what happened. Invite anyone who has their own story about that place to add it. Some of the best Jane's Walk moments come from a neighbor in the group adding a detail the leader didn't know.
7. **End at a place people can linger.** A cafe, a park bench, an ice cream shop. Walks that end at "thanks, bye" lose the relationships they could have made.
8. **Tell people about the next one.** If you only run one, that's fine. If you might run another, mention it.

## Variations

**Themed walk.** History walk. Trees and gardens walk. Public art walk. Civil rights walk. Hidden architecture walk. Disability access walk. Food walk.

**Children's walk.** Shorter route. More physical things to point at. Snacks. Best when led by older neighbors who can speak to kids about the neighborhood's past.

**Bike version.** Same recipe, longer route, slower pace than you'd think. Tour goes farther but stops feel different. Drop the "walk" in the name if you ride.

**Cross-neighborhood swap.** Two neighborhoods exchange walks. You lead one in their neighborhood, they lead one in yours. The contrast is the point.

**Recurring walk.** Some neighborhoods run a Jane's Walk monthly. Different leaders, different routes, same rhythm. Especially good in summer.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Toronto, Canada.** The first Jane's Walks happened in 2007, a year after Jane Jacobs died. Her former neighbors organized walks through the places she'd walked. The format spread quickly through the urbanist and placemaking communities.

**Globally.** Since 2007 walks have happened in over 500 cities across North America, Europe, Asia, Africa, and South America. The annual Jane's Walk Festival is the first weekend in May. The official organizing nonprofit is at [janeswalk.org](https://janeswalk.org/).

Walks have been organized by neighborhood residents, community organizations, libraries, cultural institutions, schools, and individual urbanists. Themes include public space, displacement, transit, climate, hidden histories, and dozens more.
