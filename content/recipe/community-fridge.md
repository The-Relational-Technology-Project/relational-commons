# Community Fridge

> A refrigerator in public. Anyone can leave fresh food. Anyone can take it. No paperwork.

## What it is

A working refrigerator placed in an accessible public or semi-public space, stocked by neighbors with fresh produce, dairy, prepared meals, and other perishables. It extends the [Little Free Pantry](../recipe/little-free-pantry.md) model to the cold chain. The fridge is sheltered (covered, sometimes inside a small structure), powered by a host site, and maintained by a small volunteer crew.

Community fridges have spread rapidly across U.S. cities since 2020, especially in Brooklyn, the Bronx, Washington DC, Baltimore, Los Angeles, and Oakland.

## Why it works

Fresh food is the gap that shelf-stable pantries don't fill. Most food-insecure neighbors have limited access to vegetables, fruit, dairy, eggs, and prepared meals. A community fridge in a high-foot-traffic spot, restocked daily, closes that gap directly. The model also catches restaurant overstock, garden surplus, and the half-loaf-of-bread that one household has and another could use.

The same dignity logic as a pantry: no paperwork, no hours, no question of deservingness. Open the door. Take what you need.

## What you need

- **Time:** 4 to 8 weeks to set up. Daily monitoring. 2 to 4 hours per week per steward.
- **People:** A coordinator. A maintenance crew of 3 to 8 people. A host site (business, church, community center) willing to provide power.
- **Space:** A sheltered spot with electrical access. Often a small structure ("fridge house") built to protect from rain, sun, and cold.
- **Materials:** A working refrigerator (donated, used, or new). Optional small structure to shelter it. Cleaning supplies, food storage bags, food safety thermometers, signage.
- **Skills:** Coordination, food safety basics, light carpentry for the shelter, ongoing volunteer wrangling.
- **Energy level:** Moderate to high. The daily presence is real.

## How to host it

1. **Connect with the existing network first.** Search "community fridge" plus your city. There may already be one to plug into. The infrastructure (sourcing, volunteers, food safety guidance) gets easier when you're not building alone. [Freedge.org](https://freedge.org/) maps known fridges globally.
2. **Find a host site.** A business or institution willing to provide outdoor electrical access. Examples that work: restaurants, bodegas, churches, community centers, mutual aid hubs. The host gets foot traffic. The fridge gets power and a watchful eye.
3. **Acquire the fridge.** Used works. Newer is more efficient and lasts longer. Some restaurants donate units they're replacing. A standard residential fridge with a freezer compartment is the most flexible.
4. **Build the shelter.** A simple roofed structure with sides protects from weather and adds dignity. Some collectives do beautiful murals on theirs. Permitting varies by city; small structures are often unregulated.
5. **Write your food safety guidelines.** Standard guidelines: no homemade food unless from licensed kitchens, no expired food, dated labels on donations, daily temperature checks, weekly deep clean. Post them on the fridge.
6. **Recruit a maintenance crew.** 3 to 8 people on a rotation. Each takes a day or two a week. Daily check: temperature, expired items removed, surfaces wiped. Weekly: deep clean, structure inspection.
7. **Build sourcing partnerships.** Local restaurants with end-of-day surplus. Bakeries with day-old bread. Grocery stores willing to donate near-expiry produce. Gardeners with summer overflow. A few reliable sources matter more than many sporadic ones.
8. **Make donations easy.** Post what's needed. Set drop-off hours that work for sources. Maintain a relationship with people who give regularly.
9. **Stay connected to neighbors.** A fridge is a small civic anchor. Hold quarterly meetups for stewards and supporters. Listen to what users say (with their permission).

## Variations

**Multi-fridge network.** Cities like New York have dozens of fridges with a shared coordinator group, shared sourcing relationships, and shared maintenance norms. See the [NYC Community Fridges](https://www.communityfridges.org/) network.

**Indoor version.** Some fridges live inside lobbies, churches, or community centers with extended access hours. Less exposed, slightly less accessible.

**Pantry + fridge combo.** A pantry next to the fridge. Many sites run both together.

**Kitchen-paired.** A fridge attached to a kitchen that prepares and stores hot meals for distribution. This is closer to mutual aid kitchen work; the fridge is one piece.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Brooklyn, New York.** The community fridge movement in NYC began in 2020 with a small number of fridges and grew quickly. Volunteers built shelters, sourced food, and maintained daily check-ins. The network at one point had 100+ active fridges across the boroughs.

**Washington DC, Baltimore, Los Angeles, Oakland.** Independent collectives in each city built networks during 2020 to 2022. Many remain active. The model spread without a platform, without franchising, on the strength of practical examples.

The Wikipedia entry on [community fridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_fridge) maintains a sense of the global picture. Documentation lives in many places because the practice was built by many hands.
