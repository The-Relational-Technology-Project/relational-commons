# Welcome Wagon

> A new family just moved in. Bring something over. Stay five minutes.

## What it is

The practice of welcoming new neighbors with a small gesture and useful local information. Welcome wagon practices have existed for a century in various forms, from formal chamber-of-commerce welcome baskets to a neighbor showing up with bread.

The recipe is simple. The effect is disproportionate. A neighbor who is greeted in their first week is a neighbor who knows at least one face, has one phone number, and feels like the block has noticed them.

## Why it works

Moving is hard. The first few weeks in a new place are when belonging is most fragile and most influenceable. A small welcoming gesture in that window says "you're not invisible here." That message is hard to deliver any other time.

This also creates a low-pressure reason to meet a neighbor. You're not knocking to start a relationship. You're knocking to drop something off. The relationship is the side effect.

## What you need

- **Time:** 30 to 60 minutes total. About 10 minutes at the door.
- **People:** You. Optionally a partner or a kid to come along.
- **Space:** Their doorstep.
- **Materials:** Something useful or hospitable. Baked goods, a bouquet from your garden, a gift card to a nearby restaurant or coffee shop. A one-page local info sheet (more on this below).
- **Skills:** Friendly. Brief.
- **Energy level:** Low.

## How to host it

1. **Notice the move.** Moving truck, new car in the driveway, "for sale" sign coming down. Give them a few days to land. Two to seven days after they move in is the sweet spot. Sooner feels rushed. Later feels late.
2. **Bring something simple.** A loaf of banana bread. Cookies. Fresh flowers from your yard. A small gift card (5 to 15 dollars) to a nearby spot. Not expensive. The gesture is what matters.
3. **Bring a one-page local info sheet.** This is the part that does the most work. Include: trash and recycling day, nearest grocery, nearest pharmacy, your name and number, names of a few other neighbors with their permission, a couple of favorite local restaurants, the name of the local park, the WiFi password for the porch network if there is one.
4. **Knock once. Step back.** Stand back from the door so they don't feel crowded when they open it.
5. **Introduce yourself in one sentence.** "Hi, I'm [name] from [number] across the street. I just wanted to welcome you to the block." Hand them what you brought. Smile.
6. **Don't push to come in.** If they invite you, great. If not, that's fine. Say "I'm at [number] if you need anything. There's my number on the sheet." Wave. Go home.
7. **Follow up casually.** Wave when you see them. Stop to chat for a minute when paths cross. Invite them to the next block thing that comes up.

## Variations

**Coordinated welcome.** If your block has a few connected neighbors, do a coordinated welcome. Different neighbors drop different things over the first week or two. New family meets four neighbors instead of one.

**Kid version.** If you have kids, bring them. New neighbors with kids especially appreciate this. The kids do the talking.

**Renter inclusive version.** Welcome renters as warmly as homeowners. People often skip the rental units. Don't.

**Apartment building version.** Knock on the door above and below yours. Bring something simple. Same recipe, different walls.

**Welcome basket version.** Some neighborhoods coordinate a more formal welcome basket: 5 to 10 small items contributed by different neighbors, delivered together. Beautiful when sustained but heavier to coordinate.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**The original Welcome Wagon** was a commercial organization founded in 1928 that delivered welcome baskets door to door for decades. It is largely gone in its original form, but the practice has scattered into thousands of informal versions across the country.

Many neighborhoods now run informal welcome rotations through their neighborhood association, faith community, or block-level Nextdoor or [Front Porch Forum](../reference/front-porch-forum.md) threads. Churches and faith communities often lead formal welcoming practices.

The shift from corporate welcome wagon to neighbor-led welcome is, in itself, on-thesis for this book. The practice belongs in neighbors' hands.
