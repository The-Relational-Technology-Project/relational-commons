# Bridge Meadows (Intergenerational Housing)

> A neighborhood designed around three groups who need each other: foster and adoptive families with children, low-income elders, and shared community space.

## What it is

A specific intergenerational housing model designed by Bridge Meadows in Beaverton, Oregon. About one-third of the units house adoptive or kinship families committing to three or more children from foster care. About one-third house low-income elders 55+. The remaining space supports community programming. Elders commit to volunteering at least 8 hours a week, often mentoring, tutoring, helping with meals, or providing childcare. Families and elders eat together, gather often, and weave a community across generations.

Bridge Meadows opened in 2011 in Portland. The outcomes are striking: 100% of children remain with the same caregiver and attend the same school (compared to a national average of five moves), academic performance improves for 73% of youth, and senior physical and mental health improves measurably.

## Why it works

The model addresses several interconnected crises through one design: foster care instability (kids needing consistent adult relationships and consistent schools), senior isolation (elders losing the social connection that aging in standard senior housing often produces), and housing unaffordability (both families and elders need affordable units).

The reciprocity is the engine. Elders are not "helping" families. Families are not "helping" elders. Everyone is part of one community where care moves in many directions.

## What you need

This is one of the most infrastructure-heavy recipes in the book. It is not a neighborhood-scale practice in the same way as a [block party](../reference/block-party.md). It is a real estate development with deep community design.

- **Time:** 3 to 5 years from concept to opening. Indefinite operation.
- **People:** 30 to 50 household units. Committed staff for coordination, case management, and programming.
- **Space:** Land or buildings for development. Indoor and outdoor shared community space.
- **Materials:** Construction or renovation resources. Activity supplies. Ongoing operating funding.
- **Skills:** Real estate development, nonprofit management, trauma-informed practice, intergenerational programming.
- **Energy level:** Very high during development. Moderate ongoing for residents.

## How it works

1. **Site and design.** A site large enough for about 30 to 50 units with substantial shared space. Design encourages daily interaction: walkable paths, shared outdoor space, communal kitchens and gathering rooms, units close enough that neighbors hear each other.
2. **Three-population structure.** The standard ratio:
   - One-third units for adoptive or kinship families with foster children. Families commit to fostering or adopting three or more children.
   - One-third units for income-qualified elders 55+.
   - One-third shared space and program-supporting space.
3. **Elder commitment.** Elders agree to volunteer at least 8 hours a week. This is genuine, not voluntary tourism: mentoring, tutoring, walking kids to school, sharing meals, helping with homework, childcare.
4. **Family support services.** On-site case management, counseling, workshops for families. The infrastructure that makes fostering and adopting sustainable.
5. **Community programming.** Monthly community meetings. Shared meals. Holiday celebrations. Intergenerational programs. The programming weaves the population together.
6. **Governance.** Resident voice in major decisions. Staff for coordination. Board with diverse representation.

## Variations

**Variations on the population ratio.** Some replication efforts have adjusted: more elders, fewer families, or vice versa. The fundamental insight (cross-generational design with structured reciprocity) holds.

**Senior + youth co-housing.** Less institutional versions where elders and younger families simply share housing without the specific foster care anchor.

**Adoption from foster care without housing.** The same population reciprocity (elders mentoring foster kids) can exist without a shared physical site. See [AARP's Experience Corps](https://www.aarp.org/experience-corps/) for one model.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Bridge Meadows** opened in Beaverton, Oregon in 2011 with 36 units. The results have been documented carefully:

- 100% of children remain with the same caregiver and attend the same school (compared to a national average of five moves).
- 73% of youth show improved academic performance.
- 63% show reduced mental health symptoms.
- Elder health improves noticeably.

One senior described the experience: *"The elders are providing love and support to the families, the families are doing the same, and even the children are giving back to the elders."*

The model has expanded to **Portland** and **Redmond, Oregon**, with similar outcomes. **Generations of Hope** (Champaign, Illinois) developed an earlier version of the model. Several other communities are in development.
