# Death Cafe

> An open community gathering to talk about death, dying, and what makes a life worth living. Tea and cake required.

## What it is

A free, informal gathering where people of any age, belief, or background talk about death over refreshments. No agenda. No religion. No therapy. Just honest conversation about the thing most people avoid until it's too late.

Death Cafes have happened over 23,000 times in 97 countries since the model was created in Switzerland in 2004 and popularized internationally by Jon Underwood starting in 2011.

## Why it works

Most people in most contemporary societies have no place to talk about death. Conversations about end-of-life wishes, grief, fear, meaning, and mortality are either avoided or routed through medical or religious specialists. A Death Cafe creates an everyday, social space where these conversations are normal.

The format is doing the work. Refreshments lower the stakes. The absence of agenda or expert framing means the wisdom in the room comes from the room. Strangers become unusually fast friends because of what they're talking about.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 3 hours per session. Minimum 1 month of planning and outreach.
- **People:** 1 facilitator. 5 to 20 participants.
- **Space:** A comfortable, quiet setting. A coffee shop, library, church, community center, or living room.
- **Materials:** Tea, coffee, cake or light refreshments. Comfortable seating. Optionally printed prompts.
- **Skills:** Facilitation, active listening, comfort with silence and emotion.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate. Contemplative but emotionally engaged.

## How to host it

1. **Read the [Death Cafe guide](https://deathcafe.com/how/).** It is short and useful. The model has principles: no agenda, no advertising of products or services, free or by donation, refreshments. Honor them.
2. **Pick a venue and date.** A space that feels safe and not clinical. Avoid funeral homes and hospitals for first-time attendees. Cafes, libraries, and community rooms work well.
3. **Invite broadly.** Flyer in cafes and libraries. Post in local online forums. Note that all ages, beliefs, and backgrounds are welcome.
4. **Set up the space simply.** Tables of 4 to 6 people if you have more than 8 attendees. Refreshments out. A welcoming sign.
5. **Open with the guidelines.** Confidentiality. No advice. No agenda. People share or listen as they choose. There are no right answers.
6. **Let conversation flow.** Optionally seed with one or two prompts: *What do you wish someone had told you about death? What does a good death look like to you? What unfinished business would you want to address?* But mostly, let people speak.
7. **Move tables if you want.** Halfway through, suggest people change tables to mix the conversations.
8. **Close with a brief round.** Each person, one or two sentences. What they're taking with them.
9. **Register your event.** [Death Cafe](https://deathcafe.com/) maintains a global calendar. Adding yours helps the practice grow.

## Variations

**Themed Cafe.** Around grief specifically, around medical aid in dying, around legacy, around faith traditions. Different prompt, same form.

**Online version.** Video-based. Works well. The intimacy can be surprising.

**Private version.** A Death Cafe held for a workplace, faith community, or family circle. Same format.

**Tied to a holiday.** Mexican Day of the Dead, All Souls' Day, anniversaries. The cultural framing can lower defenses.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The format was developed by Swiss sociologist **Bernard Crettaz** in 2004 as *café mortel*. **Jon Underwood** in the UK organized the first English-language Death Cafe in 2011 and built the open infrastructure that allowed the model to spread.

Today Death Cafes are held in homes, hospices, churches, libraries, hospitals, and community centers. The model is intentionally franchised in the open: anyone can hold one as long as they follow the principles. There is no fee, no franchise, no central authority. It is exactly the kind of practice this book exists to protect.
