# Talking Circle

> Sit in a circle. Pass a talking piece. Only the holder speaks. Everyone listens.

## What it is

A practice from Indigenous First Nations traditions for communication, decision-making, healing, and conflict resolution. People sit in a circle with no head position. A talking piece (feather, stick, shell, stone) moves around the circle. Only the holder speaks. Everyone else listens without interrupting, fixing, or planning a reply.

This is an Indigenous practice. It has been generously shared by First Nations elders into many contemporary contexts. When you use it, acknowledge that lineage explicitly.

## Why it works

Most group conversations are won by the loudest voice, the highest status, the most articulate. The talking circle removes all of that. The piece holds the floor. The quiet person speaks at the same volume of authority as the loud person. The listener is freed from preparing a counter-argument because they aren't going to speak next; they're going to listen now.

Over the arc of a circle, things get said that would never come out in any other format.

## What you need

- **Time:** 1 to 2 hours for a circle.
- **People:** 4 to 20 participants. A facilitator. Optionally an Indigenous knowledge keeper if appropriate.
- **Space:** Any space where people can sit in a circle without a table or barrier in the middle. Quiet. No clocks dominating.
- **Materials:** A talking piece. Often a feather, stick, shell, stone, or another object with meaning. Optionally sacred medicines if smudging is appropriate and welcome.
- **Skills:** Facilitation, deep listening, emotional presence, willingness to acknowledge origins.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate.

## How to host it

1. **Acknowledge the lineage.** Open by naming that this is an Indigenous practice. If you are on Indigenous land, acknowledge whose land. If a knowledge keeper from a local nation is willing to lead, defer to them. Don't claim authorship of a practice that isn't yours.
2. **Arrange the space.** Chairs in a true circle with no head. The talking piece in the center or held by the facilitator initially. No table in the middle.
3. **Open with a centering moment.** Silence, a breath, a brief reading, or smudging if appropriate and welcome. The opening sets the container.
4. **Explain the practice.** Whoever holds the piece speaks. Everyone else listens. You may pass when the piece comes to you. There is no fixing, advice, or interruption. The circle is sacred space.
5. **The facilitator speaks first.** Holds the piece. Speaks from the heart. Sets the depth. Then passes the piece to the next person (typically to the left, but follow local tradition).
6. **The circle moves.** Each person speaks or passes. If someone passes, they can request the piece back later. Some circles do multiple rounds: introductions, then the heart of the matter, then a closing round.
7. **Listen without preparing.** The discipline is to actually listen, not to plan a response. The piece will come to you eventually.
8. **Close.** A closing round (one word, one sentence, gratitude), a moment of silence, or a closing reading. The closing matters as much as the opening.

## Variations

**Healing circle.** Used to address harm or grief. Smaller, slower, often with a survivor's request driving the topic. See [Peacemaking Circles](../recipe/peacemaking-circles.md) for the restorative-justice extension.

**Classroom circle.** Used in schools for community-building or discipline. The kids learn the form quickly and often regulate each other.

**Decision-making circle.** A circle structured around a decision the group needs to make. Multiple rounds: information, feelings, options, decision.

**Online circle.** A video call with a clear "talking piece" convention (the speaker is muted-by-default; only they unmute). Works, with care.

## Stories from neighborhoods

Talking circles have been used since time immemorial by Indigenous peoples across the Americas. First Nations elders in Canada and the U.S. have generously shared this practice with non-Indigenous communities, knowing its potential to transform listening.

In contemporary use, circles run in classrooms, healing groups, restorative justice processes, conflict mediation, men's councils, and organizational meetings. Educators report that circles shift dynamics: quiet students find voice, dominant voices regulate themselves, listening deepens. In communities working across cultural difference, circles build bridges that other formats can't reach.

[First Nations Pedagogy Online](https://firstnationspedagogy.ca/circletalks.html) maintains a generous reference on circles as a teaching and community-building practice.
