# Creative Interventions

> A framework for responding to interpersonal violence (abuse, assault, harassment) without relying on police or the criminal legal system. Centers the survivor, mobilizes friends and family, and sometimes includes the person who caused harm in an accountability process.

## What it is

A toolkit and methodology for community-based responses to interpersonal violence, developed starting in 2004 by **Creative Interventions** in Oakland, California, out of activist communities and survivor-led movements. The toolkit gives step-by-step guidance for survivors, supporters, people who have caused harm, and broader communities.

This work belongs to a broader tradition often called "community accountability" or "transformative justice." It is closely connected to **generationFIVE**, **Bay Area Transformative Justice Collective**, and the writing of practitioners like **Mariame Kaba**, **Mia Mingus**, and others.

## Why it works

When someone is harmed by a partner, family member, or close community member, the conventional options are limited: call police (with all the risks that carries, especially for people of color, queer people, and undocumented people), enter the criminal legal system (which often re-harms survivors), or stay silent. Creative Interventions adds a third path: a deliberate, relationship-based process that centers what the survivor actually needs and works toward accountability and change.

The process is slow, hard, and relational. It doesn't always succeed. When it does, it sometimes produces change the legal system cannot: actual behavior change, repair to the survivor, and shifts in the community context that enabled the harm.

## What you need

- **Time:** Weeks to months for an accountability process. Survivor support is ongoing.
- **People:** The survivor, 2 to 8 trusted people, sometimes the person who caused harm, possibly a trained facilitator.
- **Space:** Safe, private spaces for the conversations involved.
- **Materials:** The [Creative Interventions Toolkit](https://www.creative-interventions.org/toolkit/), free online. Facilitator guides for those leading parts of the process.
- **Skills:** Deep listening, conflict facilitation, understanding of trauma and power dynamics, comfort sitting with complexity.
- **Energy level:** High. Emotional intensity. Distribute the work across multiple people.

## How to host it

This is one of the more demanding recipes in this book. The structure below is a sketch; the full toolkit is essential reading.

### For survivors

1. **You are not alone.** Identify a small circle of people you trust. The full process is not for everyone or every situation; sometimes the right step is just naming the harm to a few trusted people and being supported in your own choices.
2. **Decide what you need.** Safety. Honesty about what happened. Accountability. Distance. Repair. Continued relationship. All of these are valid; they're not mutually exclusive.
3. **You drive the process.** Nothing happens without your consent. You define what "accountability" means here.

### For friends and family supporting someone

1. **Believe them.** This is the first and most important step.
2. **Offer practical help.** Meals, childcare, housing, money, transportation, accompaniment. Don't push toward any particular choice.
3. **Don't take over.** It is not your harm. The survivor's agency drives the process.
4. **Tend to yourself.** Supporting someone through harm is hard work that can become unsustainable. Distribute the support across multiple people.

### For someone who has caused harm

1. **The toolkit has guidance for you too.** Accountability is not the same as self-flagellation, and not the same as performance. The work is: understand the impact, take responsibility, change behavior.
2. **Don't initiate contact with the survivor.** Let them set the terms.
3. **Find your own support.** Often a separate set of friends or a process facilitator. People who can hold accountability without colluding with denial.

### For the broader community

1. **Name what happened clearly.** Vague community responses produce vague accountability.
2. **Identify who should be involved.** The survivor decides.
3. **Direct conversations.** Not group pile-ons. Structured conversations with prepared participants.
4. **Build an accountability plan.** Specific commitments from the person who caused harm. Specific support for the survivor. Specific community changes that reduce future risk.
5. **Monitor and adjust.** Check in over months. Adjust the plan when needed.
6. **Evaluate honestly.** Did this work? What didn't? What did we learn for next time?

## Variations

**Pod-mapping.** A practice from the Bay Area Transformative Justice Collective. Before harm happens, identify the people in your "pod" who you could call on if you were harmed or if you caused harm. Building the pod in advance is foundational.

**Community accountability for specific harms.** Variations for sexual violence, intimate partner violence, child sexual abuse, harm in activist or organizing spaces. Each has its own literature and practitioners.

**Restorative response.** Some processes use circles (see [Peacemaking Circles](../recipe/peacemaking-circles.md)) as part of the work.

**Survivor-led groups.** Groups by and for survivors that share strategies, resources, and accompaniment without necessarily engaging the person who caused harm.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Creative Interventions** developed its toolkit through actual community accountability processes in Oakland and the Bay Area. Since publication, thousands of people have used it: activists, survivors, advocates, faith communities. The toolkit has been translated into Spanish and French, with other languages in progress.

Communities that have engaged this work report that it honors survivors more fully than conventional responses, holds people accountable more meaningfully (where it succeeds), and sometimes transforms harm rather than just containing it. They also report that it does not always succeed, that it can fail in painful ways, and that the work is not for every situation.

The broader transformative justice tradition has produced significant practitioner literature: **Mariame Kaba's** *We Do This 'Til We Free Us*, **Mia Mingus's** writings on pod-mapping, and **generationFIVE's** *Toward Transformative Justice*.
