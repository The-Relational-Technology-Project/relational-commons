# Village Movement (Aging in Community)

> A membership organization where elders stay in their homes and depend on a network of neighbors and coordinators for the support that lets them age in place with dignity.

## What it is

A consumer-driven model for aging in community. Elders pay annual dues (often $300 to $800) and receive coordinated access to volunteer help, social and educational programming, transportation, technology coaching, and referrals to vetted professionals. Members are also volunteers for each other: the reciprocity is structural.

The movement began in Boston's Beacon Hill in 2001 (Beacon Hill Village) and has grown to over 300 active Villages with many more in development.

## Why it works

The conventional choices for U.S. elders are: stay in the home you've lived in for forty years and slowly lose the supports that made it sustainable, or move to senior housing and lose the neighborhood you've spent a lifetime in. Both options are common; neither is good. The Village Movement creates a third option that keeps the home, builds the supports, and adds community.

The membership model produces an unusual feature: elders are not "served" but participate. Most members give and receive in roughly equal measure. The reciprocity is part of what keeps the model alive across decades.

## What you need

- **Time:** 1 to 2 years to establish a Village. 1 to 2 hours per week of typical member engagement.
- **People:** Member elders (typically 55+). Volunteer supporters. A coordinator or part-time staff. A board.
- **Space:** Members' homes primarily. Gathering spaces for events and programs.
- **Materials:** A volunteer coordination system. Programming materials. Transportation coordination tools.
- **Skills:** Community organizing, program coordination, volunteer management, relationship facilitation.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate for members. Higher for organizers in the launch phase.

## How to host it

1. **Connect with [the Village to Village Network](https://www.vtvnetwork.org/).** This national network supports new Villages with model documents, training, governance frameworks, and a community of established Villages willing to share what they've learned. Don't start from scratch.
2. **Form a founding committee.** 5 to 15 people, often a mix of current elders, family members of elders, professionals (social workers, geriatricians), and community organizers.
3. **Conduct community assessment.** What do older neighbors want and need? Surveys, focus groups, one-on-one conversations. The Village should fit the specific community.
4. **Choose governance.** Most Villages are 501(c)(3) nonprofits. Some are fiscally sponsored. Some have municipal partnerships.
5. **Set the membership model.** Annual dues. Sliding scale or reduced-fee membership for income-qualified members. Define services included.
6. **Build the volunteer pool.** Vetted neighbors and family members who can help with rides, light home help, technology, social calls. Background checks and basic training are standard.
7. **Launch programming.** Educational events. Social gatherings. Wellness activities. Trips. Programming builds the community that makes the help feel mutual rather than transactional.
8. **Operate and adjust.** Most Villages take 2 to 5 years to find their groove. Member feedback is the primary tool for adjustment.

## Variations

**Virtual Village.** A Village with a strong online component, useful in rural areas or for elders with mobility limitations.

**NORC (Naturally Occurring Retirement Community) Village.** A Village inside a single building or housing complex with a high concentration of elders.

**Faith-community Village.** A Village anchored in a congregation. Stronger initial trust. Sometimes narrower demographic reach.

**Multi-generational Village.** Some Villages explicitly include younger members and family caregivers. Adds capacity and complexity.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Beacon Hill Village** (Boston) launched in 2001 and is the founding model. **Capitol Hill Village** (DC), **Avenidas Village** (Palo Alto), and dozens of others have followed in major cities. **Smaller-town Villages** have grown in places like Vermont, Maine, and rural Oregon, often with stronger volunteer participation than urban versions.

The model has demonstrated outcomes in member health, social connection, and ability to age in place. Members consistently report that the value is more than the services: it's the experience of being part of something where they are seen.
