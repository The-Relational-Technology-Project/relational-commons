# Intersection Repair

> Paint a mural in the middle of a residential intersection. Add benches, a kiosk, a tiny library, a tea station. Reclaim the street as a square.

## What it is

A community-led transformation of a residential street intersection into a small public square. A large painted design covers the asphalt. Around the edges, neighbors build seating, message boards, a tiny library, sometimes a community tea station or tool exchange. The intersection becomes a place where neighbors stop instead of pass through.

The practice was pioneered by the [City Repair Project](https://cityrepair.org) in Portland, Oregon, starting in 1996. Their flagship project, **Share-It Square** at SE 9th and SE Sherrett, is the model that hundreds of others have followed.

## Why it works

Most residential streets are over-engineered for cars and under-imagined for everything else. An intersection is the most public point on a block. Paint and a few simple structures turn it from a hazard into an invitation. The act of building it together is at least as important as the result.

Intersection repair is also tactical urbanism that often becomes permanent infrastructure. Cities that initially resisted have learned that these spaces reduce traffic speed, build social cohesion, and improve neighborhood safety more than enforcement does.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 4 weeks of planning. 1 to 3 days of construction and painting.
- **People:** 5 to 50+ volunteers. A core team of 3 to 5 organizers and designers.
- **Space:** A residential intersection. Adjacent space for materials and tool storage.
- **Materials:** Paint and brushes (lots), wood for structures, basic tools, plants, mulch. Optional: bricks, pavers, signage materials.
- **Skills:** Artistic design, community organizing, basic construction, painting, negotiation with the city.
- **Energy level:** High. Sustained physical labor and creative energy.

## How to host it

1. **Get the neighbors together first.** Knock on every door on the four corners (and the adjacent block) at least twice. The project will only work if there is genuine consent. A few resistant neighbors can kill it; bringing them in early often turns them.
2. **Form a design team.** 5 to 10 neighbors who'll meet a few times to shape the design. Include kids. Include longtime residents. Include the building owners of every corner.
3. **Get city permission.** This varies enormously. Some cities have intersection repair permitting (Portland does). Most don't. A few options:
   - Apply for a "block party" permit and do it under that umbrella for one day. Some communities have kept the result and the city has accepted it.
   - Apply for a temporary art installation permit. Some cities have these.
   - Build relationships with city staff and councilmembers. Many cities will work with you if you start the conversation early.
   - Do it without permission and accept the risk. Some communities have done this; outcomes vary. Not generally recommended unless you've thought it through.
4. **Design.** The painted mural is the centerpiece. Bold geometric shapes work; so do figurative designs, mandalas, neighborhood maps, native plants, ancestral imagery. The design should reflect the specific block, not be downloaded from somewhere else.
5. **Plan the build.** Identify the side structures: a sitting bench, a tiny library, a community board, a tea or coffee station, a tool exchange. Distribute the build tasks.
6. **Hold the build day(s).** A "barn raising" weekend. Food, music, all ages. Different tasks for different skills. End each day with celebration.
7. **Identify stewards.** Who is going to maintain this? Repaint when it fades? Keep the tiny library stocked? A pair or trio of nearby neighbors usually steps up.
8. **Throw a dedication.** A block party at the new intersection. Invite the city. Invite the press.

## Variations

**Mini repair.** A single corner rather than a whole intersection. Lower bar, smaller impact, easier first attempt.

**Parking lot repair.** Some churches, schools, and community centers have under-used parking lots that become great candidates for the same treatment.

**Alley activation.** Some cities have used the same logic on alleys.

**Annual repaint.** The most successful intersections have an annual repainting tradition. The repainting is itself a [block party](../reference/block-party.md) that keeps the work alive.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**City Repair Project**, founded in 1996 in Portland, Oregon, pioneered the methodology. Their flagship project, **Share-It Square** at SE 9th and SE Sherrett, transformed a dangerous intersection into a beloved community gathering space.

Their annual **Village Building Convergence** brings together thousands of volunteers across 50+ sites for natural building, permaculture design, and intersection repairs simultaneously. Hundreds of intersection repairs have happened in Portland and in cities across North America.

The model has spread to many neighborhoods. The City Repair website maintains stories and materials.
