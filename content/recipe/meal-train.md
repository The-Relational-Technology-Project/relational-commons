# Meal Train

> Someone you know is going through something. Set up a shared calendar. Neighbors take turns bringing food for a few weeks.

## What it is

A coordinated rotation of meal deliveries for a household going through a major life event: a new baby, surgery, the death of a family member, a serious illness, or a long recovery. A few weeks of dinners delivered by friends and neighbors so the person at the center can rest, heal, and not have to think about food.

The Meal Train is one of the oldest mutual aid practices in human community. It also happens to be the easiest one to start.

## Why it works

When someone is in crisis or transition, decision fatigue is real. Even ordering food is a decision. A meal train removes that decision for weeks. It also gives the people who love them a structured way to show up. Friends who don't know what to do almost always know how to cook one meal.

The cumulative effect is more than the food. A meal train is the visible care of a community wrapping around someone. People remember it for the rest of their lives.

## What you need

- **Time:** 1 to 2 hours to set up. 20 to 40 minutes per delivery. Typically runs 2 to 8 weeks.
- **People:** 1 organizer. 5 to 20 meal contributors.
- **Space:** Recipient's home for delivery.
- **Materials:** A shared calendar (paper or digital). A platform like [Meal Train](https://www.mealtrain.com/), [Give InKind](https://www.giveinkind.com/), or [Take Them A Meal](https://www.takethemameal.com/) can help. Containers (reusable if possible).
- **Skills:** Coordination, communication, basic cooking (or willingness to order).
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate. Most contributors give one meal.

## How to host it

1. **Ask the recipient first.** Not everyone wants a meal train. Some find it overwhelming. Confirm they're up for it before starting.
2. **Gather the basics.** Dietary restrictions, allergies, food preferences, family size, drop-off instructions (porch? Doorbell? Cooler outside?), best times of day.
3. **Set up the calendar.** [Meal Train](https://www.mealtrain.com/) is free and well-designed. A shared Google Sheet works too. Add the recipient's preferences at the top.
4. **Pick the duration.** New baby: 2 to 4 weeks. Surgery recovery: depends on the surgery. Grief: 2 to 6 weeks is typical, but some communities run light meal trains for months.
5. **Pick the frequency.** Two or three deliveries a week usually works. Daily can feel overwhelming for the recipient. Weekly leaves too much gap.
6. **Send the invite.** Email, text, group thread. Include the calendar link. Make it easy to sign up.
7. **Coordinate the protocol.** Drop-off without staying, unless the recipient has explicitly said they want company. Use disposable or reusable containers (label them with names if reusable). Drop notes if you want; long visits are usually too much.
8. **Check in midway.** Are dishes adequate? Is timing working? Adjust.
9. **Close gracefully.** End on a planned date. Send a thank-you note from the recipient if appropriate.

## Variations

**Extended version.** A "low-key" meal train that delivers one meal a week for several months. Useful for long-term illness or extended recovery.

**Care train.** Beyond meals: childcare, pet care, errands, lawn mowing, snow shoveling, gift cards. Same calendar format, broader help. [Lotsa Helping Hands](https://www.lotsahelpinghands.com/) supports this format.

**Workplace version.** A meal train coordinated through a workplace for a colleague's life event. Different network, same form.

**Faith community version.** Many congregations have built-in meal train infrastructure. If you're in one, learn it. The patterns are deep.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The Meal Train predates the platform. Churches, mosques, synagogues, and informal neighborhood networks have run meal trains for generations. The platforms ([Meal Train](https://www.mealtrain.com/), [Give InKind](https://www.giveinkind.com/), [Take Them A Meal](https://www.takethemameal.com/), [Lotsa Helping Hands](https://www.lotsahelpinghands.com/)) made it easier to organize across groups that don't share a congregation, but the practice itself is older than the internet.

After a major illness, surgery, birth, or death, people who run meal trains often hear later that the meal train was what made the difference. Not because the food was extraordinary. Because someone showed up, and then someone else did, and then someone else.
