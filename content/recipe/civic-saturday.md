# Civic Saturday

> A secular gathering modeled on faith services. Songs, readings, a civic sermon, and a recommitment ritual around what it means to be a citizen together.

## What it is

A communal event that borrows from religious services to create a sacred space for civic life. Songs (often protest songs, hymns reimagined for civic purposes). Readings from civic texts (the Constitution, civil rights speeches, poems). A 20- to 30-minute "civic sermon" on a theme like citizenship, democracy, or community. A closing recommitment ritual where attendees share what they're committing to civically.

Citizen University, founded by Eric Liu, has hosted Civic Saturdays in many U.S. cities. The model is openly shareable: anyone can host one.

## Why it works

Most secular civic life lacks ritual. Voting once every two years is a moment, not a practice. A Civic Saturday creates a regular embodied experience of being a citizen with other citizens. The ritual structure does something a meeting doesn't: it treats civic participation as sacred work.

The borrowing from religious services is intentional. Religious communities have spent centuries refining how to gather a congregation around shared values, songs, texts, and silence. Civic Saturday borrows the form for civic content.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 3 months of planning. 2 to 3 hours for the event.
- **People:** 2 to 4 core organizers. 30 to 200+ attendees.
- **Space:** A community center, church, auditorium, or outdoor venue with good acoustics.
- **Materials:** Microphone, sound system, flowers, printed programs, refreshments.
- **Skills:** Facilitation, curating meaningful speakers and texts, comfort with ritual design.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. The logistics are straightforward; the emotional curation takes thought.

## How to host it

1. **Pick a theme.** What civic question are you holding for this gathering? Examples: *What do we owe each other? What does it mean to be a neighbor? What is the work of the next year?*
2. **Recruit your civic preacher.** A community member, organizer, faith leader, or thinker who can speak for 20 to 30 minutes from a personal place about the theme. Not a stump speech. A reflection.
3. **Curate the readings.** 2 to 4 short readings. Pull from civic texts (the Constitution, the Declaration, civil rights speeches, MLK's letter from Birmingham, poetry). Mix old and new. Mix expected and surprising. Local texts when possible.
4. **Choose the music.** Live music is best. A choir, a soloist, a small band. Songs people can sing along with. Protest songs, folk songs, hymns rewritten for civic purposes. *We Shall Overcome.* *This Land Is Your Land.* New songs by local artists.
5. **Design the flow.** A typical arc:
   - Welcome (5 min)
   - Opening song or poem (5 min)
   - First reading (3 min)
   - Civic sermon (20 to 30 min)
   - Second reading (3 min)
   - Silence and reflection (3 to 5 min)
   - Closing song (5 min)
   - Recommitment ritual (10 to 20 min)
   - Refreshments and gathering after
6. **The recommitment ritual.** People share, one at a time or in pairs, what they're committing to civically in the coming weeks or months. Some communities pass a candle or a token. Some have people write their commitments on cards that go into a basket. Some read commitments aloud.
7. **Promote with care.** This is a strange form to many people. Lead with the experience, not the format. "Come gather around what it means to be a citizen together."
8. **Hold it as if it's sacred.** If you treat it casually, it will feel like a meeting. The ritual is the whole point.

## Variations

**Recurring Civic Saturday.** Monthly or quarterly. Build the community of regular attendees over time.

**Civic Saturday Plus.** Add a meal afterward. The ritual carries into the food.

**Civic Saturday for emerging leaders.** Specifically designed as a platform for new leaders to give their first civic sermon. Some of the strongest sermons come from people who've never spoken publicly before.

**Themed Civic Saturday.** Around an election, a local issue, an anniversary, or a community moment.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Citizen University**, founded by **Eric Liu**, has hosted Civic Saturdays in many cities. The model has been replicated by neighborhood organizations, civic networks, and faith communities.

In one southern city, the event focused on *what it means to be a neighbor* and drew 120 people. A young Black woman from a housing advocacy organization gave the civic sermon. Attendees said they had not had a space like that in their adult lives, a place that treated civic participation as sacred. Several formed study groups afterward.

In another city, Civic Saturday became a platform for emerging leaders, deliberately handing the sermon to people who had not led publicly before.
