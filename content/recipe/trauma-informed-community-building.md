# Trauma-Informed Community Building

> Build community in a way that recognizes the wounds people are carrying. Center healing alongside organizing.

## What it is

An approach to community development that recognizes the impact of trauma (individual, collective, historical) and designs practices, programs, and physical environments to avoid re-traumatization. It operates on four principles: **Do No Harm**, **Acceptance**, **Community Empowerment**, and **Reflective Process**.

The framework has been developed especially in communities that have lived with concentrated violence, displacement, racial trauma, and economic disinvestment. Practitioners include community development organizations in cities like San Francisco, Baltimore, Memphis, and elsewhere.

## Why it works

Most community development assumes a baseline of safety, agency, and well-being that many neighborhoods do not have. Practices that work in stable middle-class settings can re-injure communities that are already carrying significant collective trauma. Asking residents to "share their stories" in a public meeting, for example, can be empowering in one context and re-traumatizing in another.

Trauma-informed community building doesn't slow the work down. It adjusts the methods so the work doesn't compound the harm.

## What you need

- **Time:** Ongoing. Planning: 3 to 6 months for serious integration. Implementation: 1 to 3 years or longer.
- **People:** Community members with lived experience of trauma. Trained facilitators and organizers. Mental health practitioners (ideally from the community). Diverse leadership reflecting community demographics.
- **Space:** Community centers, parks, cultural spaces, homes. Multiple accessible gathering spaces. Welcoming and safe.
- **Materials:** Art supplies, gardening materials, music and sound equipment, comfortable seating, culturally relevant resources. Training curricula and peer support guides.
- **Skills:** Trauma awareness, facilitation, cultural competency, listening, flexibility, systems thinking, community knowledge.
- **Energy level:** Medium to high. Emotionally and physically demanding but nourishing.

## How to host it

The four core principles guide practice:

### Do No Harm

1. **Recognize trauma in your community.** Police violence, gun violence, immigration enforcement, eviction, racial violence, sexual violence, generational poverty, historical displacement. Different communities carry different wounds. Name what's here.
2. **Audit existing practices.** What are you asking of people? Are public testimony requirements safe for everyone? Do meeting times work for people with caregiving responsibilities? Are physical spaces accessible? Is the language clinical or accessible?
3. **Avoid re-traumatization.** Stop asking "share your story" of harm without consent. Avoid surveillance practices (heavy ID checks, sign-ins, photo-taking) that mirror systems of harm. Be mindful of authority figures (police, immigration officials, child welfare workers) in spaces meant to be community-safe.

### Acceptance

4. **Meet people where they are.** Don't require people to be "ready" before participating. Don't gatekeep based on language, education, housing status, employment, sobriety, or behavior.
5. **Multiple ways to participate.** Some people will speak in meetings. Some will write. Some will draw. Some will listen. Some will help by cooking. All count.

### Community Empowerment

6. **Center those most affected in leadership.** Not as advisors. As decision-makers. Pay them. Give them real authority.
7. **Build healing into the work.** Art, movement, music, gardening, food sharing, ritual. These are not extras. They are infrastructure for processing collective experience.
8. **Train local leaders.** Mental health first aid, peer support, facilitation. Build the local capacity to hold the community's healing work.

### Reflective Process

9. **Build in regular reflection.** What's working? What isn't? Who isn't here who should be? Who is struggling? Adjust.
10. **Listen for signals.** Disengagement, conflict, burnout, and tension are often signals that something in the approach needs to shift.

## Variations

**Healing justice.** A related and broader frame, especially as developed by the **Kindred Southern Healing Justice Collective** and others. Healing justice connects individual healing to collective liberation and is rooted in Black, queer, and disabled traditions.

**Sanctuary model.** A trauma-informed organizational framework used in residential treatment, schools, and human services. Specific tools and language for whole-organization implementation.

**Cultural healing practices.** Indigenous, Black, Latine, Asian, and other cultural traditions have specific healing practices that should be centered when working in those communities. Outsiders should follow rather than lead.

**Disaster-recovery context.** Trauma-informed community building is especially relevant after major disasters or violent events. Different from baseline conditions; same principles apply.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The framework has been articulated and refined in many places. **Organizing Engagement** maintains a useful synthesis [at organizingengagement.org](https://organizingengagement.org/models/trauma-informed-community-building-model/).

Practitioners in **San Francisco** (BMAGIC, the Bayview-Hunters Point), **Memphis** (Memphis Allies), **Baltimore** (numerous community health worker programs), and elsewhere have integrated trauma-informed practice into their community organizing. Outcomes are difficult to measure on conventional metrics; the lived experience of community members consistently affirms the approach.

The **Kindred Southern Healing Justice Collective** has produced foundational resources for healing justice as a practice, distinct from clinical mental health and connected to liberation work.
