# Porch Conversations

> Sit on your front porch on purpose. Stay there long enough that someone says hi.

## What it is

The practice of being visibly present in the front of your home, ready to talk to anyone who walks by. Porch sitting is the smallest, lowest-energy practice in this book. It requires nothing. It produces, over time, more neighbor relationships than almost anything else.

Front porches existed because pre-air-conditioned homes were too hot to sit in during summer evenings. People sat outside because they had to. Out of that necessity grew the social infrastructure of pre-television neighborhoods. The porch was where you found out what was happening, who was sick, who needed help, who'd had a baby.

You can rebuild that infrastructure. It just takes sitting down.

## Why it works

Most of why people don't know their neighbors is structural. Cars carry us into garages. Phones absorb the time we used to spend outside. The result is that we live in the same place as twenty other households and never see any of them.

Porch sitting reverses the structural default. If you are visibly outside, neighbors who pass will eventually say hi. If they say hi enough times, they will start to slow down. If they slow down enough times, you will know their names. The whole thing takes weeks. It does not require any plan.

## What you need

- **Time:** Anywhere from 20 minutes to a few hours. Best when it becomes routine.
- **People:** You. One chair is enough.
- **Space:** A front porch, stoop, or street-facing yard. Anywhere visible to people walking by.
- **Materials:** A chair. Coffee or tea or a beer. Optionally a book or a project. Optionally a sign that says "come say hi."
- **Skills:** Hospitality. Comfort with small talk. Willingness to be interrupted.
- **Energy level:** Low. This is the practice you can do when you have no energy for anything else.

## How to host it

1. **Put a chair where people can see you.** This is the entire setup. If you have a porch, use it. If you have a stoop, sit on it. If you have a driveway, drag a chair to the end of it.
2. **Set a time you'll do this regularly.** Friday afternoons. Sunday mornings. Weekday evenings before dinner. Routine matters more than duration. Neighbors will start to know you'll be there.
3. **Bring something to do with your hands.** A book, a beverage, a small project. The point is you're available but not staring at every passerby. People who feel watched walk faster.
4. **Smile and say hi to people who pass.** Don't push. A nod, a wave, a hi. That's enough. They'll stop when they're ready.
5. **When someone stops, ask one real question.** Not "how are you" but "have you lived here long" or "do you know what's blooming in that yard." A real question gets a real answer.
6. **Don't keep them.** Two minutes the first time is fine. Five minutes the third time is great. Friendship moves at the speed of slow.
7. **Notice patterns.** Same dog walker every morning. Same family with the stroller. Same kid on the skateboard. Over weeks, you'll start to know who lives where.
8. **Invite someone in eventually.** When a porch acquaintance becomes a porch friend, the next move is a real conversation inside or in the backyard. That's how it deepens.

## Variations

**Coordinated porch hour.** Several neighbors on the same block agree to be on their porches Friday afternoons from 5 to 7. Suddenly the block is alive at the same time. Conversations start to flow between porches.

**Porch sign.** A small sign that says "porch is open" or "come say hi" or "free coffee" lowers the social cost of stopping. Some neighbors put out a thermos and cups.

**Porch project.** Knit, whittle, sketch, garden. Visible activity invites curiosity. People will ask what you're doing.

**Apartment version.** No porch? Sit in the lobby. Sit in the courtyard. Sit on the building's front steps. Same idea, different infrastructure.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Front Porch Republic** is both a publication and a loose movement of communities reclaiming porch culture. Their core insight: physical design shapes social possibility. Porches close to the street invite neighboring; suburbs designed around garages prevent it.

Across the country, individual blocks and faith communities have organized intentional porch hours where neighbors agree to be visible at the same time. The simplest version of this practice has been catalogued at [the Front Porch Republic's writings](https://www.frontporchrepublic.com/2017/07/sitting-on-the-porch/).
