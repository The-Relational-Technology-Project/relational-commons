# Resilience Hub

> Take a trusted local space that is already a community anchor. Add backup power, supplies, training, and partnerships. Now it's also a disaster coordination point.

## What it is

A community facility that serves neighbors year-round as a gathering space and resource center, and shifts into a coordination and support center during disasters. Rather than building separate emergency infrastructure that sits unused 95% of the time, Resilience Hubs enhance existing trusted institutions with backup power, supplies, and partnership networks.

The model has been formalized by the [Urban Sustainability Directors Network](https://www.usdn.org/resilience-hubs.html) and is being deployed in cities including Baltimore, Seattle, Los Angeles, Portland, and many others.

## Why it works

In a crisis, people go where they already feel safe. They don't suddenly learn to trust an emergency-only government facility. They go to their church, their community center, their library, their neighborhood cultural center. The Resilience Hub model accepts this reality and resources the places people already trust.

The "year-round + emergency" framing is the structural insight. A hub that only existed in emergencies would atrophy. A hub that runs programming all year, with backup capacity, is exercised infrastructure when it matters.

## What you need

- **Time:** 6 to 12 months to establish. Ongoing coordination.
- **People:** 5 to 10 community leaders. Volunteers. A staffed facility partner.
- **Space:** An existing community facility, 1,500+ square feet, with accessibility.
- **Materials:** Backup power (solar plus battery is ideal). Emergency supplies. Communication equipment. Programming infrastructure.
- **Skills:** Community organizing, project management, emergency response knowledge.
- **Energy level:** High to set up. Moderate to sustain.

## How to host it

1. **Identify the right facility.** Look for a place that is already community-trusted and community-led. Churches, cultural centers, community centers, libraries, food banks, and neighborhood nonprofits all work. Avoid converting a building that isn't already woven into the neighborhood.
2. **Assess what exists.** What programming runs there now? Who shows up? What hours? What's the relationship to the neighborhood?
3. **Identify the gaps.** What would be needed to function during a crisis?
   - Backup power. Solar plus battery is increasingly standard.
   - Communication. Satellite, ham radio, charged backup phones, WiFi backup.
   - Stockpiled supplies. Water, first aid, food, medication storage, blankets.
   - Climate control. Cooling and heating capacity that can run on backup power.
   - Sanitation. Bathroom function during power outages.
4. **Build the partnerships.** A hub works because it's networked. Local food bank for emergency food. Public health for medical coordination. Mutual aid pods for community response. City emergency management for official coordination. Faith communities for volunteer mobilization. Build these relationships in calm times.
5. **Run year-round programming.** Classes, meetings, celebrations, food distribution, social services drop-ins. The programming is what keeps the hub alive and trusted.
6. **Train.** Hub staff and volunteers should be prepared: first aid, mental health first aid, basic emergency response, communication protocols.
7. **Practice.** Annual drills are common. A planned outage to test the backup power. A scenario walkthrough of a heat wave or earthquake.
8. **Activate during emergencies.** When a crisis hits, the hub shifts: extended hours, expanded supplies distribution, coordination with mutual aid, shelter capacity if needed, information distribution.

## Variations

**Climate Resilience Hub.** Specifically focused on heat, cold, wildfire smoke, and storm response. Includes air filtration, HVAC capacity, and climate-specific supplies. See the [CREW (Climate Resilience Education and Work)](https://climateresolve.org/resilience-hubs/) network.

**Multi-hub network.** Multiple hubs across a city or county that coordinate. Some cities are building toward "no neighbor more than a mile from a hub."

**Cultural anchor hub.** A hub explicitly built around a cultural community (Black church, Korean community center, Latine cultural space). The cultural specificity is part of what makes it a hub for the people it serves.

**Hub-lite.** A smaller version with limited backup capacity but strong programming and community trust. Some grocery stores, libraries, and small businesses serve this role informally.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Seattle.** Lake City Collective opened the **qwiʔqwuʔ center** (meaning "little water" in Lushootseed) in 2020. It hosts cultural celebrations, community gardens, tutoring, and serves as an emergency hub.

**Los Angeles.** The Boyle Heights Arts Conservatory operates as a resilience hub seven days a week. Their manager noted that they "were a resilience hub before we were designated one because we're grassroots, community engagement." That captures the pattern: the strongest hubs grow organically from authentic community work rather than top-down planning.

**Baltimore, Portland, Oakland, Miami, and others** have built city-supported networks of community-led hubs in recent years.
