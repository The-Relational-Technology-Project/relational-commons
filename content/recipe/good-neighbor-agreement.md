# Good Neighbor Agreement

> Write down what neighbors and a nearby business or institution want from each other. Sign it. Check in regularly.

## What it is

A non-binding written agreement between a neighborhood and a nearby business, institution, or development. It addresses specific concerns through a structured negotiation and produces a document both parties sign. The agreement is not a legal contract. It is a public, mutual understanding that gives both sides a way to hold each other accountable without lawyers.

Good Neighbor Agreements work for bars, dispensaries, shelters, light industrial operations, large developments, religious institutions, and any other place where the operations of one party affect the daily life of nearby neighbors.

## Why it works

Most neighborhood-business conflicts run on rumor, complaint, and resentment until someone calls the city. A Good Neighbor Agreement replaces that loop with a different one: specific asks, specific commitments, regular check-ins, and a paper trail that everyone references.

The document itself matters less than the process of making it. Neighbors who sit at a table with business owners learn that the business has constraints. Business owners who sit at a table with neighbors learn what their operations actually feel like at 11pm. Both sides leave with relationships they didn't have before.

## What you need

- **Time:** 3 to 6 months for the initial negotiation. Ongoing annual or quarterly check-ins.
- **People:** A neighborhood organization with standing in the area. 3 to 5 community organizers. Representative residents. Business leadership willing to come to the table.
- **Space:** A neutral meeting space. Library, community center, faith space. Not the business's location and not a hostile neighbor's home.
- **Materials:** A template agreement (sample below). Notes from listening sessions. Written documentation of the final terms.
- **Skills:** Negotiation. Active listening across multiple perspectives. Willingness to translate between groups that don't share vocabulary.
- **Energy level:** Moderate to high. Emotionally demanding to hold both perspectives, especially if there's history.

## How to host it

1. **Start with a neighborhood organization that has standing.** This is essential. A few angry neighbors writing a document does not carry weight. A neighborhood association, block club, or faith community standing behind the process does.
2. **Run listening sessions with neighbors first.** Two or three small meetings (8 to 15 people each) where residents describe their concerns in specific detail. Not "the bar is loud" but "Friday and Saturday after 11pm we hear shouting that wakes the kids." Specificity is the whole game.
3. **Document everything in writing.** Names attached to issues are stronger than anonymous complaints. With consent.
4. **Invite the business or institution to a negotiation meeting.** A formal letter signed by the neighborhood organization. Explain the process. Frame it as collaboration, not confrontation. Most businesses say yes if asked respectfully.
5. **Convene the first meeting.** Neighborhood reps, business leadership, a neutral facilitator if possible. Each side explains their perspective, their constraints, and what they want.
6. **Identify the specific issues.** List them. Group them. Most agreements address: operating hours, noise management, parking, waste disposal, local hiring or sourcing, security and safety, communication channels for when issues arise.
7. **Draft the agreement.** One section per issue. Specific commitments on both sides. Mention the regular check-in schedule. Keep it readable: 2 to 5 pages, not 20.
8. **Both parties sign.** A public signing is good. Some neighborhoods invite local press. The publicity is part of the accountability.
9. **Hold the check-ins.** Quarterly is best for new agreements, annual once it's stable. The check-in is the difference between an agreement that works and a piece of paper in a drawer.

## A simple template

```
GOOD NEIGHBOR AGREEMENT
Between [Neighborhood Organization] and [Business or Institution]
Signed [date], to be reviewed [frequency]

1. ISSUE: [e.g., Late-night noise]
   Commitment from [business]: [specific action]
   Commitment from neighbors: [specific action, often "first contact business directly before escalating"]

2. ISSUE: [e.g., Parking]
   ...

[Continue for each issue]

CHECK-IN SCHEDULE
- Next check-in: [date]
- Frequency: [quarterly / annual]
- Convened by: [organization]

CONTACT
- Business contact: [name, phone, email]
- Neighborhood contact: [name, phone, email]

SIGNED
[signatures of both parties]
```

## Variations

**Development version.** A larger version used during the planning approval process for a new development. Often includes commitments on traffic mitigation, affordable housing percentages, local hiring, community benefits funding.

**Institutional version.** For schools, hospitals, religious institutions. Tends to focus on traffic, noise, events, and how the institution shows up in neighborhood life.

**Pre-emptive version.** Some neighborhoods negotiate Good Neighbor Agreements with businesses *before* problems develop, especially when new businesses are opening. The agreement starts the relationship on solid footing.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**CHUN (Capitol Hill United Neighborhoods)** in Denver has facilitated Good Neighbor Agreements since 2010. Their archive of agreements with restaurants, dispensaries, and shelters is one of the strongest practice records in the country.

**Park Tavern**, a bar in a residential Capitol Hill neighborhood, was generating late-night noise concerns. Instead of letting hostility build, the bar proposed a Good Neighbor Agreement. The negotiation produced: an 11pm noise cutoff on weeknights, designated parking areas, a resident contact person, and quarterly check-ins. The bar also hired from the neighborhood and created a community advisory group. Years later, when a new building added residents nearby, the bar proactively suggested updating the agreement.
