# Babysitting Co-op

> A group of neighbor families who trade childcare hours using points. No money changes hands. Kids grow up knowing the whole block.

## What it is

A small group of families who watch each other's children on a points-or-tokens system. Each family earns points by sitting and spends them when they need care. The math is simple. The relationships are the real product.

Babysitting co-ops have been quietly running for decades in some neighborhoods. They save families hundreds to thousands of dollars a year. More importantly, kids grow up with multiple trusted adults nearby. That changes everything.

## Why it works

A regular sitter costs a lot and is one relationship. A co-op costs nothing and embeds your kids in a network of caring adults who know them, their schedules, their allergies, and their bedtime rituals. The relationships flow both ways: the co-op becomes the social spine of the families involved.

The points system removes awkwardness. Nobody owes anyone a favor. Everyone is participating in a balanced exchange.

## What you need

- **Time:** 1 to 2 hours of initial planning. 10 to 15 minutes per exchange. Quarterly meetings.
- **People:** 4 to 12 families with kids, geographically close enough to walk or drive easily.
- **Space:** Members' homes.
- **Materials:** A tracking system. A shared spreadsheet, poker chips, or printed "babysitting bucks." All work.
- **Skills:** Light facilitation, communication, flexibility.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate, distributed across the group.

## How to host it

1. **Find 4 to 8 interested families.** Pitch it at a [block party](../reference/block-party.md), a parent group, a school pickup. Look for families whose kids are similar ages or who get along.
2. **Hold a setup meeting.** Decide your points system together. A common version: each family starts with 20 points. One hour of sitting = 1 point (per family, not per child). Night hours after 9pm count double. Multiple kids count 1.5x. Holidays optional.
3. **Pick a tracking method.** A shared Google Sheet is easiest. Poker chips are charming. Printed "babysitting bucks" feel like real money. Use what your group likes.
4. **Write a one-page guidelines doc.** Cancellation policy (24 hours notice), what happens if someone falls into significant point debt, food/snacks expectations, bedtime norms, emergency contacts.
5. **Run your first exchange.** Pick a family who needs a date night first. The cycle begins.
6. **Hold quarterly meetings.** Address frictions, balance issues, invite new families if you want to. Use the meeting to deepen relationships, not just to do logistics.
7. **Welcome new families slowly.** Each new family needs to be vouched for. Two existing families is a good rule. Trust scales with shared experience.

## Variations

**Sleep version.** Extends to overnight care for older kids. Higher trust threshold, much higher value when it works.

**Day-care swap.** Two families with similar schedules trade days. Lower-key, doesn't need a points system, works for two families that click.

**Cross-block version.** A co-op that spans a few blocks rather than one. More families, slightly more driving.

**Inter-co-op gatherings.** Larger gatherings where multiple co-ops bring kids together. Builds the network of networks.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Montana State University student housing.** Families formed a co-op with nothing but poker chips and monthly meetings. Chips exchanged at each sitting. Worked beautifully for a population that had little money and a lot of need.

**East Coast co-ops.** Some have run for decades with 20 to 30 families. Sophisticated systems. Printed bucks. Annual potlucks. Long histories of kids who grew up across each other's houses and now organize co-ops of their own with their kids.

The average family in an active co-op saves an estimated $500 or more a year. The harder thing to measure is what it means for a kid to grow up knowing four other families well enough to feel at home in them.
