# Transition Town

> A community organizing approach where neighbors take responsibility for their own resilience: local food, local energy, local economy, local skills.

## What it is

A global movement of communities building resilience and reducing dependence on fossil fuels. Transition groups form locally, identify their community's specific priorities, and organize around them: community gardens, repair cafes, renewable energy cooperatives, local currencies, skill-shares, food coops.

The movement started in 2006 in **Totnes, UK**, with the work of **Rob Hopkins** and others, and now spans 67+ countries. It is intentionally distributed: each Transition group is autonomous, working from a shared framework but adapted to local conditions.

## Why it works

Climate change, energy uncertainty, supply chain fragility, and economic precarity all argue for stronger local capacity. Transition Towns is one of the longer-running models for community organizing around that argument. It is not survivalist; it is built on relationships and shared work, with the understanding that resilience and connection grow together.

The framework is intentionally light. There is no central authority. Local groups pick from a menu of "ingredients" (originally 12, now 43) and apply what fits.

## What you need

- **Time:** Long arc. Most successful Transition groups operate over years and decades.
- **People:** A founding group of 5 to 20 committed neighbors. Working groups for specific projects.
- **Space:** Meeting space (homes, community centers, libraries). Specific project sites (gardens, workshops, distribution points).
- **Materials:** Whatever each project requires. Shared documents, communication channels, project-specific equipment.
- **Skills:** Community organizing, project management, technical skills relevant to specific projects (gardening, building, electrical, etc.).
- **Energy level:** Moderate. Distributed across many people and projects.

## How to host it

1. **Connect with the [Transition Network](https://transitionnetwork.org/).** They support new initiatives with materials, training, and a global network of practitioners.
2. **Form a founding group.** 5 to 10 neighbors committed to the work. Mix of perspectives and skills.
3. **Pick your initial focus.** The movement's 43 ingredients are a menu, not a checklist. Common starting points:
   - Community gardens or food forests.
   - A repair cafe or skill-share series.
   - Local food initiatives (CSAs, farmers markets, community kitchens).
   - Energy descent or efficiency projects.
   - A local currency or time bank.
   - Community-supported agriculture or producer coops.
4. **Run "awareness raising" alongside action.** Film screenings, talks, walks, workshops. The events that bring more neighbors into the conversation.
5. **Build working groups.** Each project gets its own working group of 3 to 10 people. The main initiative coordinates, supports, and connects them.
6. **Partner widely.** Local government, schools, businesses, faith communities, neighborhood associations. Transition Towns is meant to be a connector, not a competitor.
7. **Document and celebrate.** Each project completed, each garden bed dug, each repair night, each meal cooked together. The visibility carries the next group of participants in.
8. **Sustain over time.** Many Transition Towns experience cycles of high energy and lower energy. The strongest groups have learned to operate at multiple paces and to welcome people back after long absences.

## Variations

**Sector-specific.** Some communities run only the food piece (a Transition Food Hub) or only the energy piece (a Transition Energy Coop) without taking on the full framework.

**Neighborhood scale within a city.** Larger cities sometimes have multiple Transition groups by neighborhood, plus a citywide coordinating body.

**Local economy focus.** Some groups have led with local currency, time banks, or producer cooperatives as the unifying project.

**Cohousing connection.** Some Transition Towns have spawned [cohousing](../recipe/cohousing.md) projects as the next step in committing to shared local life.

**Faith-based variant.** Some congregations have organized around Transition principles. The faith framing provides additional moral language and member commitment.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Totnes, UK**, the founding Transition Town (2006), has built community gardens, a renewable energy cooperative, a local currency (the Totnes Pound), a community brewery, and many other projects across two decades.

**Transition US**, the U.S. hub, supports hundreds of Transition initiatives across the country. Notable examples include Transition Sebastopol (California), Transition Pittsburgh, and many smaller-town initiatives.

**Globally**, the movement has spread to 67+ countries with thousands of local initiatives. Each looks different. The underlying commitment to community-led local resilience is the constant.

The model has also informed adjacent movements: solidarity economy networks, eco-villages, climate adaptation organizing, food sovereignty movements. The boundaries between these are porous.
