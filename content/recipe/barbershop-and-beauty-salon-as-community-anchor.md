# Barbershop and Beauty Salon as Community Anchor

> Recognize, support, and partner with the third places that already do the relational work of your neighborhood. Often they are barbershops and salons.

## What it is

Not a new thing to build, but a recipe for noticing and partnering with what already exists. In many neighborhoods, especially Black neighborhoods, barbershops and beauty salons function as community anchors: spaces where people get personal care and also where they get advice, information, mentorship, conflict mediation, and sometimes intervention in crisis. The barber or stylist often knows the regulars' family situations across years.

The HAIR Campaign (Health Advocates In-Reach and Research) and related health partnerships in Baltimore and other cities have built formal collaborations with barbershops to deliver health information through these existing trust networks.

## Why it works

Trust does not arrive on demand. Barbershops and salons have decades of accumulated relationship. When public health, social service, or community organizing work tries to reach a population, the question is not "how do we build trust" but "where does trust already live." Often the answer is in the chair and at the wash basin.

The same logic applies to bodegas, laundromats, corner stores, taco trucks, and other small local businesses. The barbershop is the most documented case. The pattern is broader.

## What you need

- **Time:** Months to years of relationship-building. No shortcuts.
- **People:** A patient organizer, public health worker, or community member. The shop owner and stylists themselves.
- **Space:** The shop itself.
- **Materials:** Whatever the partnership requires. Posters and printed material if it's a health collaboration. Books and references if it's literacy work. Talking points if it's voter education.
- **Skills:** Listening, respect for the shop's culture and rhythms, willingness to follow rather than lead.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate. The relational work is what it is.

## How to host it

1. **Get a haircut. Or get your hair done.** Become a customer. Don't show up as a project. The shop is a workplace and a community space, not an outreach venue.
2. **Listen.** Over months of visits, you'll start to hear what the shop sees in the neighborhood. Who's struggling. What's coming up. What's broken. This is intelligence that no survey produces.
3. **Build the relationship with the owner or lead stylist.** They are the gatekeepers. If they don't trust you, no partnership will work.
4. **Offer something real.** Don't show up empty-handed. Examples of what shops have actually wanted:
   - Posters about local services (drug treatment, housing, voter registration).
   - A regular "office hours" by a public health worker, social worker, or attorney.
   - A small stipend to display materials or distribute resources.
   - A "barbershop talks" event series where the shop hosts conversations.
   - Connection to other shops doing similar work.
5. **Follow the shop's lead.** The shop knows what works for its clientele. Bring options; let them choose what fits. If they say no to something, that's information, not a problem.
6. **Recognize the labor.** Stylists who do this work are doing emotional labor alongside their professional labor. Acknowledge it. Pay for it when you can.
7. **Don't extract.** This is the easy mistake. Researchers, public health programs, and community organizations have all sometimes used barbershops as a "channel" without giving back. Don't.

## Variations

**Health partnership.** The HAIR Campaign model. Specific health information (blood pressure screening, mental health resources, prostate cancer awareness) delivered through trusted shops.

**Voter outreach.** Some shops host voter registration tables and become trusted intermediaries for civic information.

**Mentorship programs.** Shops have anchored youth mentorship programs where older clients mentor younger customers, often around school, work, and decision-making.

**Other anchor businesses.** The same logic applies to many neighborhood institutions: bodegas and corner stores, laundromats, taco trucks, taquerias, neighborhood bars, hair-braiding spots, nail salons. Each has its own dynamics. The recipe adapts.

## Stories from neighborhoods

The **HAIR Campaign** (Health Advocates In-Reach and Research), based in Baltimore and other cities, has built formal partnerships between universities, public health departments, and barbershops to deliver health information through these existing trust networks. Outcomes have been substantial across multiple health domains.

The **Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture** has documented [the roles of barbershops and beauty salons](https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/community-roles-barber-shop-and-beauty-salon) as community anchors in Black communities historically and today.

In neighborhoods across the country, barbers and stylists also do informal violence interruption work, mediate family conflicts, and refer people to services. This labor is often invisible in policy discussions about community safety and well-being.
