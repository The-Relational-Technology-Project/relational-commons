# Little Free Pantry

> A small weatherproof cabinet of nonperishable food and supplies, placed somewhere visible. Take what you need. Leave what you can.

## What it is

A small structure stocked by neighbors and accessible to anyone, any hour, with no paperwork or eligibility check. People leave cans, pasta, rice, hygiene products, diapers, school supplies. People take what they need. The model is direct, dignified, and runs on a gift economy.

The first Little Free Pantry was built in 2016 by Jessica McClard in Fayetteville, Arkansas, who saw Little Free Libraries and thought: what if we did this with food. There are now thousands of them across North America.

## Why it works

The barriers to food access in most cities are not just about money. They're about hours, paperwork, transportation, and the stigma of asking. A pantry on a sidewalk with no door, no staff, and no question of who deserves it removes all of those barriers at once.

The pantry also gives neighbors a near-zero-friction way to give. You drop off a bag of cans on your way to work. The whole loop closes in a way that food drives and 501(c)(3) intake forms cannot match.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 3 weeks of setup. 1 to 2 hours a week to maintain.
- **People:** 1 to 2 stewards. 5 to 10 neighbors willing to stock regularly.
- **Space:** A high-traffic, accessible spot. Sidewalks, church grounds, library lawns, school edges. Permission from the property owner.
- **Materials:** A small weatherproof cabinet. Repurposed bookcase, custom build, or a kit. Sturdy enough to last a year. Plus initial stock: canned goods, pasta, peanut butter, soap, menstrual products, baby supplies.
- **Skills:** Light carpentry or repurposing. Basic communication. Willingness to be the contact person.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate. Mostly the stocking and weekly check.

## How to host it

1. **Find the right spot.** Visible from the street. Reachable on foot. Safe to access at night. Talk to the property owner: a church, school, library, business, or a neighbor with a corner lot. Get written permission.
2. **Check zoning.** Most cities allow these as accessory structures, but a quick call to your city or a permit search avoids surprises.
3. **Build the structure.** Three options. Buy a kit ($150 to $400, search "little free pantry kit"). Repurpose a bookcase, old cabinet, or newspaper box, weatherproofed. Build from scratch. Whatever it is, it should have a roof, a door, a shelf or two, and weatherproofing.
4. **Stock it.** First fill: canned vegetables, fruit, soup, beans, peanut butter, pasta, rice, granola bars, hygiene products (soap, toothpaste, toothbrushes, menstrual products), basic kitchen items (Ziploc bags, plastic utensils). Skip anything that needs refrigeration (for that, see [Community Fridge](../recipe/community-fridge.md)) or anything that can melt or expire fast.
5. **Add signage.** "Take what you need. Leave what you can." Plus your contact info as the steward. Optionally a printed list of common items needed.
6. **Promote.** Post in neighborhood forums, Buy Nothing groups, church bulletins, library boards. Both for users and for donors.
7. **Maintain weekly.** Once a week, walk over. Remove damaged items, expired food, and anything that looks unsafe. Restock the gaps. Note what runs out fastest. Some pantries become very busy; others stay quiet. Both are fine.
8. **Build a stocking team.** 5 to 10 neighbors who commit to dropping off items regularly. A simple group text or shared sheet helps coordinate.

## Variations

**With fridge.** A community fridge can be co-located with a pantry for perishables. See [Community Fridge](../recipe/community-fridge.md).

**Apartment building version.** Inside a lobby or laundry room. Lower visibility but reliable supply chain.

**School version.** Schools that host pantries report high uptake by families who would never apply for food assistance.

**Themed pantry.** Hygiene products only (menstrual products are particularly hard to access). Pet food. School supplies in August. Holiday-themed stocks.

**Bilingual version.** Signage in the languages spoken in your neighborhood.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Jessica McClard** in Fayetteville, Arkansas. May 2016. A $250 microloan. The first Little Free Pantry on her church grounds. The idea spread without a platform, without a franchise, without a fee. Today there are thousands across North America.

The movement proved especially vital during COVID-19. Cities that had a few pantries in 2019 had dozens by 2021. Many have stayed.
