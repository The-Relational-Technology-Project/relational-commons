# Interfaith Bridge

> Bridging across religious and spiritual traditions through structured dialogue, shared service, and the long work of getting to know each other's lives.

## What it is

A loose family of practices for building relationships across religious and spiritual difference. Interfaith dialogue circles, joint service projects, study groups across traditions, shared meals, and structured curricula like Interfaith America's BRIDGE program for campus interfaith engagement.

The work is older than the term. Religious communities have practiced cross-tradition dialogue and shared service for centuries. Contemporary movements have built more visible infrastructure and curricula.

## Why it works

Religious difference is one of the deepest fault lines in many communities. It's also one where people often share more than they realize: a commitment to care for the stranger, to honor the dead, to mark sacred time, to feed each other. Practices that put people from different traditions into shared room and shared work surface those overlaps without erasing the real differences.

The work is not "we're all really the same." It's closer to "we are different in ways that matter, and we can still do this together."

## What you need

- **Time:** Varies dramatically. A single dialogue: 90 minutes. A semester-long curriculum: months. A long-running interfaith partnership: years.
- **People:** Faith leaders or lay leaders willing to organize. Participants from multiple traditions.
- **Space:** Often rotates across the participating communities' spaces (mosque, church, synagogue, temple). Neutral spaces also work.
- **Materials:** A discussion guide or curriculum. [Interfaith America](https://www.interfaithamerica.org/) and others provide many. Food that honors dietary practices across traditions.
- **Skills:** Facilitation across difference. Willingness to handle awkward moments. Respect for what each tradition considers sacred.
- **Energy level:** Moderate.

## How to host it

1. **Start with leaders, not events.** Before any public event, spend time with the leaders of the participating communities. One-on-one conversations. Build the relationships that will hold the work together when something goes wrong.
2. **Decide the form.** Common forms:
   - **Dialogue circles.** Small group conversations on shared themes (hospitality, grief, justice, holy time).
   - **Joint service.** A shared project (a community garden, a winter coat drive, a hot meal program).
   - **Study group.** Reading sacred texts from each other's traditions together, often led by clergy or scholars from each.
   - **Visits.** Attending each other's services as observers.
   - **Curricula.** Programs like [Interfaith America's BRIDGE](https://www.interfaithamerica.org/curricula/bridge/) for campus contexts.
3. **Set respectful agreements.** Confidentiality. No proselytizing. Permission to ask questions, including hard ones, with humility. Permission to say "I don't want to answer that yet."
4. **Honor practices and constraints.** Dietary laws. Prayer times. Holy days. Gender-segregation traditions in some communities. Schedule and food planning needs to reflect these from the start.
5. **Let the form go deeper over time.** A first dialogue is introductory. By the fifth or tenth, real depth becomes possible. Be patient with the arc.
6. **Move toward shared work.** Dialogue alone has a ceiling. Shared service builds different kinds of trust. Most strong interfaith partnerships have both.
7. **Acknowledge difference.** Don't paper over genuine theological or political disagreement. Sit with it. Surface what you can, and live with what you can't.
8. **Pass it on.** Stronger interfaith work involves the next generation. Bring in young people. Train new facilitators.

## Variations

**Refugee resettlement.** Interfaith partnerships often grow out of joint refugee resettlement work. Sponsoring a family together is a long-term shared project that builds relationship through shared responsibility.

**Civic interfaith.** Faith communities organizing together on issues like housing, immigration, climate, or racial justice. The shared work is the bridge.

**Spiritual but not religious.** Many contemporary "interfaith" spaces include people who are spiritual but not affiliated with a tradition. Communities like [Nuns & Nones](https://www.nunsandnones.org/) bridge religious and post-religious spiritual seekers.

**Campus interfaith.** Structured programs for universities and colleges. [Interfaith America's BRIDGE Curriculum](https://www.interfaithamerica.org/curricula/bridge/) is the most-used resource.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Interfaith America** (founded by Eboo Patel as Interfaith Youth Core) has built one of the largest interfaith networks in the U.S., training thousands of young leaders and producing extensive curricula.

**Parish Collective** has supported neighborhood-rooted Christian communities in interfaith partnerships across the U.S. and internationally.

**Nuns & Nones** has built relationships between Catholic women religious and unaffiliated millennial spiritual seekers around shared commitments to justice and contemplative life.

Many of the most durable interfaith partnerships started locally and quietly: a rabbi and an imam meeting for coffee for ten years, a church and a mosque sharing a building, a Sikh community and a Methodist church running a food pantry together.
