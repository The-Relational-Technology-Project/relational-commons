# World Cafe

> A structured way for 25 to 2,000 people to actually have a conversation, not sit through a presentation.

## What it is

A dialogue method where small groups (4 to 5 people each) sit at café-style tables and discuss an open-ended question. After 20 to 30 minutes, everyone rotates to a new table except one "table host" who stays. The host welcomes the new group and shares what was said. Ideas cross-pollinate. After 2 or 3 rounds, the whole room comes together to "harvest" what emerged.

The World Cafe was developed in the 1990s and has been used in thousands of community gatherings, organizational strategy sessions, and global summits.

## Why it works

Most "community engagement" meetings are one mic and a hundred people. A handful of confident people speak. Everyone else listens or leaves. The World Cafe inverts that: everyone speaks, no one dominates, and the rotation builds a shared sense of what the room is actually thinking.

The intimacy of a small table makes people speak. The rotation prevents any one table from becoming an echo chamber. The harvest produces collective insight that didn't exist in any one head before the session.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 4 hours including setup and harvest.
- **People:** 25 to 2,000+. Smaller is more intimate; larger gets more diversity.
- **Space:** Large room or outdoor area with many small tables and room to move.
- **Materials:** Small round tables (4 to 5 chairs each), tablecloths, butcher paper for the tabletops, colored markers, flowers, water, a microphone for harvest.
- **Skills:** Good question design, light facilitation, comfort with ambiguity.
- **Energy level:** Moderate. The logistics are the work; the hosting is light once people understand the format.

## How to host it

1. **Design the question.** This is the most important part. The question should be open-ended, matter to the people in the room, and not have a single right answer. Examples: *"What does our neighborhood need?" "What would have to be true for this to work?" "What's at stake for you in this?"* Avoid yes/no questions, leading questions, or questions disguised as advocacy.
2. **Set up the room like a real café.** Multiple small round tables. Tablecloths. Butcher paper covering each table. Markers in colored cups. Flowers in jars. Music as people arrive. Light food and drinks if you can. The atmosphere does real work.
3. **Welcome and frame.** 10 minutes. Welcome people. Explain the format. Read the question aloud. Set the etiquette: listen with curiosity, speak from experience, draw or write on the paper if it helps.
4. **Round one.** 20 to 30 minutes. People talk at their tables. Encourage drawing on the paper. Facilitator does not need to interrupt unless tables are silent.
5. **Rotate.** Everyone moves to a new table. One person (the "table host") stays at the original table and welcomes the newcomers with a brief summary of what was discussed. Rotation usually takes 2 to 3 minutes; build in transition time.
6. **Round two.** 20 to 30 minutes. New group builds on what the host shared.
7. **Optional round three.** Especially useful when the question is layered or complex.
8. **Harvest.** Bring everyone back together. 30 to 45 minutes. Ask: "What themes emerged? What surprised you? What actions are you ready to take?" Write the harvest where everyone can see. If you have a graphic recorder, even better.
9. **Send people home with something.** A photo of the harvest. A summary by email. The butcher paper from their table. The continuity matters.

## Variations

**Two-question cafe.** Round one and two each have a different question, building on each other. Useful for problem-then-solution arcs.

**Mixed-stakeholder cafe.** Intentionally seat people who don't usually talk to each other (residents and elected officials, students and faculty, organizers and police). The rotation forces continued mixing.

**Online cafe.** Zoom breakout rooms with rotation between rooms. Works, with stronger facilitation. Use shared docs in place of butcher paper.

**Mini cafe.** 8 to 15 people, two or three tables, two rounds. The format scales down.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**A divided neighborhood**, in a city where police-community relations were strained, hosted a World Cafe with 80 residents around the question "How do we want to live together?" Small tables mixed police, organizers, longtime residents, and young people.

In the first round, people mostly restated their positions. By the second round, a police officer heard a mother describe her fear for her son. By the third round, the conversations had texture and humanity that the room hadn't had before. The harvest surfaced themes of accountability, mutual understanding, and shared investment in kids' safety. Participants said they wouldn't have heard each other without the structure.

The original framework was developed by Juanita Brown and David Isaacs. The method has been used in over 80 countries.
