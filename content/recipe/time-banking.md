# Time Banking

> A community-scale system where neighbors trade hours of help. One hour = one credit. A haircut equals an hour of childcare equals an hour of tax help.

## What it is

A formal exchange system where everyone's time is valued equally, regardless of what they do with it. Members offer skills and services. Members request what they need. Hours are tracked. Credits earned by giving can be spent by asking.

Time banking has its modern roots in the work of Edgar Cahn in the 1980s, who built it as a tool for community development and an alternative to charity. Today there are thousands of time banks worldwide.

## Why it works

A time bank fixes one of the most stubborn problems in mutual aid: the imbalance between people who give a lot and people who are afraid to ask. The shared currency (hours) makes asking neutral. You aren't owing anyone a favor; you're spending what you earned.

The flat-hour value (everyone's hour counts the same) is the radical move. It refuses the market's hierarchy. The doctor who needs help moving and the mover who needs help reading a medical form trade hours one for one.

## What you need

- **Time:** 1 to 3 months to establish. Ongoing indefinitely.
- **People:** 10 to 30 founding members. Grows organically.
- **Space:** Minimal. Members' homes. Occasional community gathering space.
- **Materials:** A tracking system. [TimeBanks.org](https://www.timebanks.org/) offers software (hOurworld is the main open platform). A spreadsheet works for small banks.
- **Skills:** Relationship building. Light coordination. Conflict resolution when exchanges go sideways.
- **Energy level:** Low to moderate. The administrative work is real but distributed.

## How to host it

1. **Recruit 10 to 20 founding members.** People who share enough trust to make the first exchanges feel safe. Mix of life stages, skills, and needs.
2. **Hold a launch meeting.** Explain the model. Each member writes a short list of what they can offer and what they might want. Examples: home cooking, dog walking, basic plumbing, tutoring, computer help, listening, garden work, transportation, hair cuts.
3. **Pick a tracking system.** [hOurworld](https://www.hourworld.org/) is the most-used open platform. Some banks use Time and Talents or in-house spreadsheets. Whatever you pick, it should let people see offers and needs and log hours.
4. **Set the agreements.** One hour = one credit. Each member gets a starting balance (often 5 to 10 hours) so people can begin asking immediately. Cancellation norms. Confidentiality. What happens if someone goes into significant negative balance.
5. **Run the first exchanges.** A member posts a need. Another member responds. They arrange the help. Afterward, the hours are logged.
6. **Hold monthly gatherings.** Potlucks, skill-shares, celebrations. The bank is more than logged hours. The relationships are the substance.
7. **Build a steward team.** 2 to 4 people who manage onboarding, troubleshoot stuck exchanges, and run the gatherings.
8. **Welcome new members slowly.** Each new member should be introduced. Trust scales with shared experience.

## Variations

**Senior-focused.** Many of the most successful time banks center older adults. They have time. They have skills. The bank reduces isolation. See the Member Connect models that AARP and others have run.

**Cross-organization.** A bank that includes both individuals and small organizations (a community garden, a school, a youth program). Hours flow both ways.

**Domain-specific.** A skill-share-only bank. A childcare-only bank (similar to a [Babysitting Co-op](../recipe/babysitting-co-op.md), but more formalized).

**Hybrid.** A time bank that also accepts traditional volunteer hours, donations, or in-kind contributions. More complex; works when the founding team has experience.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Edgar Cahn**, a legal scholar, founded the modern time banking movement in the 1980s as a tool for community development, especially in places where traditional volunteer programs missed the people they meant to serve. [TimeBanks USA](https://www.timebanks.org/) (now succeeded by the [TimeBanks Network](https://www.hourworld.org/)) has supported thousands of banks since.

Reports from active time banks consistently show two things: members save $300 to $1,200 a year through service exchanges, and (more importantly) older adults in time banks report 90% more new friendships through participation. The hours are real but they aren't the point.
