# Placemaking

> A community-led process for transforming a public space. Observe, listen, experiment with cheap quick changes, learn, iterate.

## What it is

A method for designing public space that puts the people who use the space at the center of the design process. Developed and codified by the [Project for Public Spaces (PPS)](https://www.pps.org/), founded in 1975. Placemaking can be applied to plazas, parks, streets, waterfronts, libraries, transit stops, and almost any public space that isn't quite working.

The signature move is "lighter, quicker, cheaper": test ideas with temporary, low-cost interventions before committing to expensive permanent changes. A cardboard chair will tell you something a rendering can't.

## Why it works

Most public space design is done by experts, presented to communities for "input," then built. The result is often a space that nobody uses despite being technically well-designed. Placemaking inverts the order. The community observes, dreams, and tests first. The experts come in to support what the community has discovered.

The "lighter, quicker, cheaper" principle is the practical engine. A movable chair, a temporary parklet, a pop-up cafe, a painted crosswalk: each one teaches the community what they actually want, faster than any planning process can.

## What you need

- **Time:** 3 to 12 months for an initial transformation. Ongoing stewardship indefinite.
- **People:** Community members. Local organizations. City partners. 30+ engaged in the core work.
- **Space:** The public space being transformed. Meeting venues for design sessions.
- **Materials:** Camera or notes for observation. Flip charts for visioning. Paint and basic materials for experiments. Photography documentation.
- **Skills:** Community engagement, observation, facilitation, design thinking, project management.
- **Energy level:** Moderate to high. Includes both physical activity and strategic thinking.

## How to host it

The PPS framework moves through five steps:

1. **Meet the community and identify stakeholders.** Who uses this space? Who lives near it? Who doesn't use it that you'd want to? Map the stakeholders. Hold initial conversations. Listen.
2. **Assess the site and its assets.** Spend time there. PPS recommends visiting at different times of day, days of the week, weather conditions. Notice: where do people stop? Where do they pass through? What's broken? What's working but not enough? What does the space want to be?
3. **Create a vision.** Convene the community to articulate what the space could become. Use a [World Cafe](../recipe/world-cafe.md), [Appreciative Inquiry](../recipe/appreciative-inquiry.md), or charrette format. The vision is the destination; the path will be iterative.
4. **Develop an implementation plan with "lighter, quicker, cheaper" experiments.** Don't try to build the final state immediately. Pilot pieces:
   - Movable chairs in a plaza.
   - A pop-up cafe or food truck.
   - A temporary parklet (a parking space turned into seating and planting).
   - Painted crosswalks or sidewalk extensions.
   - A community garden bed.
   - Music or programming during certain hours.
   Each experiment is a learning opportunity. Document what works.
5. **Manage ongoing activation.** A space that's been transformed needs continued care. Programming, events, maintenance, evolution. Build the community of stewards.

## Variations

**Tactical urbanism.** A faster, sometimes guerrilla version of placemaking. Smaller-scale interventions, sometimes done without permission, that demonstrate what's possible. [Tactical Urbanists' Guide](http://tacticalurbanismguide.com/) is a strong resource.

**Permanent placemaking.** When experiments have proven a direction, more permanent capital investment follows: better seating, lighting, plantings, programming staff.

**Internal placemaking.** Some institutions (libraries, schools, hospitals) apply placemaking principles to their own spaces. Same methodology, smaller scope.

**Equitable placemaking.** Placemaking that specifically addresses displacement, gentrification, and the history of who has been excluded from public space. PPS and others have produced significant materials on this.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Project for Public Spaces** has worked on hundreds of public space transformations since 1975. Some of the better-known: **Bryant Park** in New York City, **Detroit's Campus Martius Park**, the **Times Square** redesign, public market revivals in Pike Place (Seattle), Reading Terminal (Philadelphia), and others.

At smaller scale, the methodology has been used by neighborhood associations, business improvement districts, libraries, and grassroots groups across the country.

The **Great Places** index that PPS has maintained for decades is a useful reference for what well-functioning public space looks like.
