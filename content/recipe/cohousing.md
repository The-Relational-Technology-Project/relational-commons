# Cohousing

> A neighborhood designed for community. Private homes around shared indoor and outdoor space. Residents shape the design and run the place.

## What it is

A model of intentional, collaborative neighborhoods that combines private homes with shared infrastructure: common kitchens and dining rooms, shared laundries, gardens, recreation spaces. Residents participate in the design and ongoing governance. Most cohousing communities have between 12 and 50 households. Shared meals are a typical practice; many communities cook together two or more nights a week.

Cohousing emerged in Denmark in the 1970s and has spread internationally. The [Cohousing Association of the U.S.](https://www.cohousing.org/) supports the movement here.

## Why it works

The standard American housing pattern: detached homes, no shared space, drive-everywhere infrastructure. The pattern produces isolation by design. Cohousing inverts the design choices: pedestrian paths, smaller private homes balanced by larger shared spaces, common meals, governance structures that require residents to actually meet each other.

The result is a neighborhood where you know the people who live around you, where kids range freely across the development, where you might not cook every meal yourself, where shared resources (lawn equipment, guest rooms, tools) reduce both costs and waste. Residents report higher quality of life and stronger community than they could achieve in conventional housing.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 5 years to develop a new cohousing community. Ongoing community management indefinitely.
- **People:** 8 to 50+ households. A committed founding group during development.
- **Space:** Land for development. Architectural design that supports shared and private space.
- **Materials:** Design guides, legal templates, governance tools. Significant financial resources for development.
- **Skills:** Community development, group facilitation, project management, financial planning. Often supplemented by professional cohousing consultants.
- **Energy level:** Very high during the development phase. Medium for ongoing community life.

## How to host it

This is one of the most resource-intensive recipes in the book. The path is not a weekend project; it is a multi-year community development effort.

### If you're considering starting a new community

1. **Visit existing cohousing.** The [Cohousing Association](https://www.cohousing.org/find-cohousing/) maintains a national directory. Visit several communities. See what daily life looks like. Talk to residents about what worked and didn't.
2. **Connect with the network.** Cohousing has decades of practitioner knowledge. The [Cohousing Association](https://www.cohousing.org/) hosts conferences, provides consultants, and connects forming groups with established ones.
3. **Form a founding group.** Five to ten committed households as a core. This group will do years of work together before any building exists. Compatibility matters.
4. **Engage professional support.** Most successful new cohousing engages cohousing consultants and architects with cohousing experience. The model has specific design and process needs.
5. **Find land.** Often the hardest part. Urban sites are scarce; rural and suburban sites are more common. Look for sites near transit, schools, and existing community.
6. **Design together.** The "participatory design" phase is unique to cohousing. The future residents make design decisions: layout, density, common spaces, units, sustainability features. The process typically takes a year or more.
7. **Finance.** Construction loans, member deposits, sometimes municipal support. The financing is complex; professional advice is essential.
8. **Build, move in, govern.** Move-in is the start, not the end. Cohousing communities run their own governance: regular meetings, work sharing, decision-making, shared meals.

### If you're considering joining an existing community

1. **Visit.** Most communities welcome visits, often through a structured process.
2. **Stay for a meal.** Shared meals are the heart of most cohousing. Eating with the community is the best way to see what daily life is.
3. **Understand the commitment.** Cohousing requires ongoing participation. Meetings, work shares, conflict navigation. Some people thrive in this. Some don't.
4. **Apply formally.** Existing communities have processes for new members. Be patient; the screening is mutual.

## Variations

**Senior cohousing.** Specifically for older adults. Often paired with aging-in-place support structures (similar to [Village Movement](../recipe/village-movement-aging-in-community.md)).

**Multigenerational cohousing.** Explicit design for families across generations to live together.

**Cohousing in retrofit.** Some existing apartment buildings have been converted to cohousing or near-cohousing models.

**Pocket neighborhoods.** A lighter variant: small clusters of homes oriented around a shared courtyard or common space. Less institutional than full cohousing.

**Eco-village.** A cohousing-adjacent model with stronger environmental design goals. See the [Foundation for Intentional Community](https://www.ic.org/).

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Cohousing communities** exist across the U.S. and internationally. Examples include **Glacier Circle** (Davis, CA, one of the first U.S. senior cohousing developments), **Earthaven** (North Carolina), **Cambridge Cohousing** (Massachusetts), **Eno Commons** (North Carolina), and dozens of others.

Residents consistently report increased social capital, friendships across generations and backgrounds, shared caregiving, and reduced isolation. Established communities have demonstrated long-term viability across decades.

The model is not without challenges: governance disputes, the difficulty of selling units, the labor of ongoing community work. Most cohousing residents describe these as worth the trade.
