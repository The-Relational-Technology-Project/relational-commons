# The Longest Table

> One very long table. Hundreds of neighbors. One meal.

## What it is

A free public meal at one continuous outdoor table. Neighbors arrive with potluck dishes (or just themselves) and sit down with people they've never met to eat together. The visual is the point: a 500-foot table down the middle of a street says something about a place that no flyer can.

The Longest Table is what happens when a [block party](../reference/block-party.md) grows up and decides to invite the whole neighborhood.

## Why it works

Scale changes what's possible. A 20-person dinner is intimate. A 500-person dinner is civic. People who would never sit at a small table with strangers will sit at a long one, because the table itself is the invitation.

The long table also signals welcome in a way that a fenced event cannot. No registration. No ticket. No dress code. If you are walking past, you can sit down.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 4 weeks of organizing. 2 to 3 hours for the event. 2 to 3 hours of setup and cleanup.
- **People:** 2 to 3 core organizers. A dozen volunteers for setup, food coordination, and cleanup. 50 to 1,000+ participants.
- **Space:** A public outdoor space. A closed street works best. A park or plaza works if the table can be built end-to-end.
- **Materials:** Many tables (folding works), many chairs, plates, utensils, cups, napkins, tablecloths, potluck dishes. Sound for opening remarks. Waste and recycling stations.
- **Skills:** Event coordination at scale. Volunteer wrangling. Calm in the face of weather.
- **Energy level:** Moderate to high. The day-of logistics are real. The planning is lighter than the event suggests.

## How to host it

1. **Find your space.** A street works best for the visual but requires a permit. A plaza or park can work and is easier. Look for somewhere with foot traffic, parking access, and shade or shelter if your climate needs it.
2. **Get the permits.** Street closure, public gathering, and possibly food service depending on jurisdiction. Start this conversation 6 to 8 weeks out.
3. **Build the organizing team.** 2 to 3 people who will own different pieces: logistics, communications, food coordination. Avoid one person trying to do all of it.
4. **Recruit volunteers.** Reach out 4 weeks out. You need helpers for setup, food intake, water station, cleanup. A volunteer per 25 attendees is a reasonable ratio.
5. **Publicize.** Local Nextdoor or [Front Porch Forum](../reference/front-porch-forum.md). Posters in cafes, libraries, schools. Local newsletter. Word of mouth on each block. Emphasize: free, open to anyone, bring food if you can, just show up if you can't.
6. **Set up the table.** Folding tables end to end. Place settings every two feet. A few feet of space between table sections for movement. Decorate simply if you decorate at all (flowers in jars).
7. **Run a simple rhythm.** Brief welcome and thanks. People serve themselves potluck. Mingling and eating for 90 minutes. A short closing word before things break up. Don't over-program.
8. **Document something.** A photographer or a few volunteers with phones. The image of the long table is what travels and brings people back next year.
9. **Clean up together.** Volunteers stay. Many attendees will help if you ask. The street should be cleaner than you found it within 90 minutes of the meal ending.

## Variations

**Indoor version.** Long table down the center of a community center, school gymnasium, or church hall. Works in cold climates.

**Sectioned version.** If you can't fit one continuous table, build it in 30-to-50-foot segments with walkways between. Maintain the visual.

**Themed version.** Build the meal around a theme that fits your neighborhood (harvest, lunar new year, founding anniversary). Don't over-theme.

**Smaller version.** 50 to 100 people, one long table in a park. Lower stakes, easier to start.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**Chelsea, New York City.** During the isolated early months of 2020, Maryam Banikarim envisioned a communal gathering inspired by shared meals in Egypt. She posted the concept on Nextdoor and received an overwhelming response. Co-founder Andy Lerner joined. The first Longest Table on West 21st Street drew 500 neighbors to a 544-foot table made of 68 connected smaller tables.

By recent events, more than 1,000 neighbors gather. 90% of attendees report feeling more positive about their neighborhood afterward. The model has been replicated in Harlem, Kansas City, California, and Paris.
