# Free Store

> A physical space where neighbors leave things they don't need and take things they do. No money, no bartering, no questions.

## What it is

A storefront, warehouse, market stall, or pop-up where goods circulate as gifts. People drop off what they're done with. People take what they want. The store runs on volunteers and a gift-economy ethic. Free Stores are sometimes permanent fixtures and sometimes monthly "Really Really Free Markets."

The model goes back at least to the Diggers in 1960s San Francisco. Recent revivals include the [Free Store Project](https://thefreestoreproject.org/) in New York City (founded 2020) and the Really Really Free Markets distributed across North America.

## Why it works

A Free Store is a [Buy Nothing](../recipe/buy-nothing-group.md) group with a physical address. The advantages: no internet required, no membership process, anyone can walk in, and the goods you encounter aren't a curated feed. The downside compared to Buy Nothing is the building costs (space, hours, volunteers). When the space exists, the store also becomes a hangout, a community anchor, and a place where the unhoused, the new arrival, and the curious longtime resident can encounter each other on equal footing.

## What you need

- **Time:** 2 to 4 months to launch a permanent store. 4 to 6 weeks for a one-day Really Really Free Market.
- **People:** 4 to 10 core organizers and volunteers. 20+ regular participants.
- **Space:** A physical storefront, warehouse, basement, garage, or outdoor space.
- **Materials:** Shelving, tables, signage. Optional: tracking system for high-value items, security camera if the location requires it.
- **Skills:** Coordination, basic retail logistics, outreach, gentle conflict resolution.
- **Energy level:** Medium. Consistent volunteer presence is the recurring cost.

## How to host it

### Option A: Really Really Free Market (one day)

1. **Pick a date and a public space.** A park, plaza, parking lot, or community center. Permit if required.
2. **Recruit 5 to 10 organizing volunteers.** Set up, intake, browsing assistance, takedown.
3. **Spread the word.** Bring items you don't need. Take items you want. Everything free. No barter, no sale.
4. **Set up tables by category.** Clothes, kitchen, books, toys, electronics, miscellaneous.
5. **Run the market for 3 to 6 hours.** Encourage browsing and conversation.
6. **At the end, donate remainder.** Take whatever's left to a thrift store, mutual aid distribution, or community group that can absorb it.

### Option B: Permanent Free Store

1. **Find space.** Donated retail space, church basements, warehouse corners. Many Free Stores have started in spaces an organization already owned and wasn't using.
2. **Set hours.** Twice a week is a typical starting cadence. Weekend hours catch people who work.
3. **Recruit a steady volunteer team.** 8 to 15 people on a rotation. Each volunteer covers 2 to 4 hours a week.
4. **Set policies.** What you'll accept and what you won't. Most stores refuse: broken electronics, stained or torn clothes, anything with safety issues, anything illegal. Post the list at intake.
5. **Organize the store.** Keep it browsable. Rotate stock. Don't let it become a hoarder space; that's the most common failure mode. Have a "donate to next user" pile that goes out weekly.
6. **Build relationships with donors and partners.** Local thrift stores can refer customers to you. Local moving companies can bring things they'd otherwise dump.
7. **Address misuse openly.** Some people will treat a Free Store as a discount supply for reselling. Most stores handle this lightly: per-visit limits, a chat with frequent offenders. Heavy-handed responses kill the trust that makes the store work.

## Variations

**Mini Free Store inside a community center.** A corner with a shelf or two. Lower stakes. Lower throughput.

**Mobile Free Store.** A van or trailer that pops up at neighborhood events.

**Themed Free Store.** Clothes only. Books only. Kid stuff only.

**Hybrid with Repair Cafe.** A Free Store that includes a fixing day, so items can be repaired before being given away.

## Stories from neighborhoods

**The Free Store Project** (New York City, founded 2020 by Myles Smutney) operates a permanent storefront on the Lower East Side and has since spread to other cities. Their model includes a focus on accessibility, a refusal to track who takes what, and an explicit framing as mutual aid rather than charity.

**Really Really Free Markets** have happened in dozens of cities across North America. The format is open: anyone can run one. Documentation lives in zines, blog posts, and the institutional memory of the people who've thrown them.

**The Diggers** in 1960s San Francisco ran free stores as part of a broader gift-economy practice in the Haight-Ashbury neighborhood. The contemporary movement is in a real lineage with that work.
