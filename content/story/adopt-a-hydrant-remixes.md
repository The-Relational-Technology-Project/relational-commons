# Adopt-a-Hydrant and Its Descendants

> A tiny Rails app let Bostonians claim a hydrant to shovel out — and got re-skinned within months for Honolulu's tsunami sirens and, later, San Francisco's named storm drains. Small apps encoding one relationship remix best.

In 2011, Code for America fellow Erik Michaels-Ober built Adopt-a-Hydrant for Boston: a map of the city's fire hydrants where a resident could claim one and pledge to shovel it out after snowstorms. That's the whole app. It encoded exactly one civic relationship — *this piece of shared infrastructure is mine to care for* — and Code for America open-sourced it.

The remixes came fast. Honolulu's IT deputy director Forest Frizzell re-skinned it in 2012 as **Adopt-a-Siren** for tsunami sirens, with residents reporting failures during monthly tests. Chicago did sidewalks; multiple cities did storm drains. San Francisco's utility launched **Adopt-a-Drain** in 2016 with the crucial upgrade of *naming rights*: 25,000 drains with stewards and names like Drainy McDrainface and Drainmond Green — and the program nearly doubled in 2023, because whimsy is a retention mechanic, not decoration.

One honest caveat traveled with every remix: the code alone never spread it. Each city needed a champion — a fellow, an IT deputy, a utility program manager — proving again that the deployment is a relationship even when the app is twenty files.

What it teaches: the adopt-a-thing pattern is the most reliably remixable shape in civic tech, and it's block-sized by nature: tree wells, benches, little libraries, community fridges — anything that needs a person gets one, by name.
