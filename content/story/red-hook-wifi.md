# Red Hook WiFi: The Network That Held When Sandy Hit

> A Brooklyn youth nonprofit started a rooftop mesh network in 2011. When Hurricane Sandy knocked out everything else, the neighborhood-owned WiFi stayed up — and FEMA plugged into it, not the other way around.

The Red Hook Initiative — a youth nonprofit in a low-lying, geographically isolated Brooklyn neighborhood dominated by the Red Hook Houses — began building a small rooftop mesh network in November 2011 with the Open Technology Institute, running open-source firmware, maintained by local young people.

Eleven months later Hurricane Sandy drowned the neighborhood's power, phone, and internet. The little mesh stayed up. RHI's building became the hub where neighbors charged phones and reached relief — and FEMA connected its satellite links *into* the community's network to reach residents. In the aftermath, the improvised arrangement formalized into the **Digital Stewards** program: neighborhood young adults paid and trained to build, test, and maintain the network, which expanded with solar-powered rooftop nodes and still operates.

Detroit's Community Technology Project scaled the same model citywide: its Equitable Internet Initiative trains and pays digital stewards — residents like Monique Tate in the North End and Nyasia Valdez in Southwest Detroit — to run neighborhood wireless networks anchored by trusted local institutions, with curricula published for anyone to reuse.

What it teaches: resilience is a social outcome. The network mattered in disaster because a trusted neighborhood institution owned it and local people were paid stewards of it. The remixable unit is not the routers — it's the steward role.
