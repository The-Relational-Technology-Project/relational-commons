# Trade School, New York to Everywhere

> In 2009, a vacant shop on the Lower East Side became Trade School — where anyone could teach and attendance cost a barter item the teacher requested.

<p>In 2009, a small group of people who ran a skills-sharing website called OurGoods — Rich Watts, Louise Ma, and Caroline Woolard — were given the opportunity to use a vacant shop on New York's Lower East Side. They had no budget and no institutional backing. What they had was an idea: a school where anyone could teach, and the price of attendance was a barter item the teacher requested. For just over a month, over 800 people participated in more than 70 classes. The barter items were playful and intimate — jars, music tips, vegetables, help finding an apartment. The classes ranged from sewing to sign language to web design. The only qualification for teaching was passion and knowledge. No credentials required.</p><p>What Trade School surfaced was a hunger not just for learning but for exchange that felt human. As co-founder Caroline Woolard observed, the expansion of Trade School chapters around the world may have been driven by the feeling that some things just don't work in a market economy — that people have a fundamental need for creativity, connection, and the dignity of having something to offer. The organizers insisted on horizontal governance: every Trade School chapter operated as a collective or cooperative. Trade School grew from one vacant storefront into a global network that ran for a decade, from 2009 to 2019. Chapters sprouted across the US, Canada, and beyond. They published a book documenting the project. And then they stopped.</p><p>The decision to end was as intentional as the decision to begin — a recognition that the form had run its course, and that clinging to an institution can become its own kind of extraction. What Trade School left behind is a model: that learning happens best in relationship, that everyone is an expert in something, and that the barter — the act of bringing something you have to offer — transforms a student into a participant.</p>
