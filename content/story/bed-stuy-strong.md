# Bed-Stuy Strong: Slack, Airtable, and a Phone Number

> A handmade sign invited Bedford-Stuyvesant neighbors into a Slack group as COVID hit. With Airtable, volunteer-written bots, and a deliberately phone-first hotline, ~28,000 neighbors got groceries — and the network stayed.

In March 2020, Sarah Mathews posted a handmade sign in Bedford-Stuyvesant inviting neighbors into a Slack workspace. Within a week it held a thousand people; at its height, Bed-Stuy Strong moved about $1.2 million in grassroots donations into a week's groceries for roughly 28,000 neighbors, prioritizing elderly, disabled, and immunocompromised people in a historically Black neighborhood hit early and hard.

The stack was deliberately humble: Slack for coordination, Airtable for intake tickets split across the neighborhood's four quadrants, a Google Voice hotline for neighbors without internet, and a thin layer of volunteer-written bots (Alyssa Dizon and George Farcasiu among the engineers) routing tickets into channels where nearby volunteers claimed them. Farcasiu's design principle deserves engraving: 'We're looking at the social system we have and augmenting and enabling that.' The hotline wasn't a fallback — it was an equity decision.

When the crisis ebbed, the network didn't: working groups turned to vaccine access, free public Wi-Fi, letters to incarcerated neighbors, and small-business preservation.

What it teaches: off-the-shelf tools plus a little glue code can run a several-thousand-person neighborhood logistics operation — and a mutual aid moment, handled relationally, leaves behind exactly what the organizing shelf calls power: relationships, lists, practiced people, and infrastructure looking for its next job.
