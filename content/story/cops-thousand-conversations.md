# COPS: A Thousand Conversations Before the First Meeting

> Ernesto Cortés Jr. came home to San Antonio in 1974 and did over a thousand relational meetings before founding COPS — which then packed city hall, held the microphone, and won the west side its drainage, sidewalks, and two billion dollars of infrastructure.

Communities Organized for Public Service was not founded at its founding convention. It was founded in more than a thousand one-on-one relational meetings that Ernesto Cortés Jr., freshly trained by the Industrial Areas Foundation, ran through the Catholic parishes of San Antonio's Mexican-American west and south sides in 1974. The IAF's Ed Chambers — who systematized Saul Alinsky's craft into teachable practice — called the relational meeting 'the most radical thing we teach': thirty to forty-five minutes, face to face, probing another person's interests, stories, anger, and talent.

What the conversations surfaced wasn't an issue an organizer imported — it was flooding. West-side residents documented that drainage bonds had been approved for their neighborhoods for decades and never spent there. They built a 'counter-budget' of capital projects, and in August 1974 packed roughly five hundred members into City Council chambers, where leader Beatrice Gallego held the microphone until the council heard them out. First president Andy Sarabia — a civilian Air Force employee and parish leader — embodied the model: leadership from within the constituency, accountable to it.

Over fifty years COPS/Metro has won drainage, paved streets, sidewalks, parks, libraries, a west-side natatorium, the shift to single-member council districts that ended minority-rule politics in a majority-Mexican-American city, the Project QUEST job-training program, and by its own accounting helped direct some two billion dollars in infrastructure bonds to neighborhoods that had been promised them for generations.

What it teaches: the founding meeting is the last step of founding. Institutions people already trust — parishes, congregations, schools — are organizing infrastructure. And the IAF's iron rule rides through every practice on this shelf: never do for people what they can do for themselves.
