# Citizens' Assemblies: From Ancient Athens to Your Town Hall

<p>The idea that a randomly selected group of ordinary citizens can make better decisions than elected officials sounds radical until you realize it's one of the oldest ideas in democracy. Athenian democracy ran on sortition — selection by lottery — for most public offices. The concept resurfaced in the 1970s when Ned Crosby in Minnesota and Peter Dienel in Germany independently invented the citizens' jury: a small group of randomly selected people, given time and information, deliberating on a public question. For decades, the practice remained niche. Then, starting around 2010, something shifted. Ireland used citizen deliberation — first a constitutional convention, then a citizens' assembly — to break decades-long deadlocks on marriage equality and abortion, questions that had paralyzed elected politicians. France convened 150 randomly selected citizens to deliberate on climate policy. And across dozens of cities and regions, from Australia to Belgium to Oregon, variations of the model began appearing with increasing frequency.</p><p>By the mid-2020s, citizens' assemblies had been used over 600 times at local, regional, national, and international scales. In 2024, DemocracyNext — a research and advocacy organization — published a comprehensive Assembly Guide distilling the practical wisdom of this growing movement into a step-by-step manual. The guide covers everything from how to recruit participants by democratic lottery (send invitations to thousands of random addresses, then stratify for demographics), to how to structure deliberation days (learning phases, small-group discussion, plenary sessions, drafting recommendations), to what happens after the assembly ends (follow-up, member aftercare, institutionalization). The guide is honest about what makes assemblies work: participants need at least forty hours together to move past surface positions and find genuine common ground. Anything less, and you get a focus group dressed up as democracy.</p><p>What the citizens' assembly movement reveals is that the bottleneck in democratic life is rarely a lack of good ideas — it's a lack of good process. When you give a representative cross-section of people the time, information, and facilitation to deliberate seriously, they consistently produce thoughtful, nuanced recommendations that reflect the complexity of real tradeoffs. The assembly model is not a replacement for representative democracy but a complement to it — a way of bringing the lived experience and common sense of ordinary people into decisions that are too important or too contentious for politicians alone. The relational technology here is the process itself: the lottery that ensures no one is excluded, the facilitation that ensures everyone is heard, and the time that ensures depth over speed.</p>
