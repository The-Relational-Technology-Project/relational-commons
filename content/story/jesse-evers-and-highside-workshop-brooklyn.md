# Jesse Evers and Highside Workshop, Brooklyn

> Jesse Evers ran a multipurpose warehouse in Brooklyn hosting concerts, art, and motorcycle repair — discovering that trust is the prerequisite for belonging.

<p>For a few years, Jesse Evers ran a space in Brooklyn called Highside Workshop. It was a multipurpose warehouse that hosted concerts, art galleries, motorcycle repair workshops, tattoo popups, band practices, and people just hanging out. He estimates they ran close to a hundred events. When Jesse got the warehouse, he had no plan — just a conviction that his creative and talented friends needed a place to do their thing. Space is hard to come by in New York City, and he spent the first six months gutting and rebuilding the space before inviting anyone in.</p><p>What Jesse discovered — and later wrote about in a guide that has circulated widely among community builders — is that the relationships between a community space and its members are fundamentally different from those of a business. If you build the right conditions, people show up not as customers but as co-creators. They contribute time, money, and energy in ways that don't make economic sense but make perfect relational sense. Trust, Jesse found, is the prerequisite for belonging — the feeling that you have every right to be somewhere without needing permission. Making that feeling real required making the space vulnerable, being generous beyond what was transactional.</p><p>He learned that leaving room for serendipity mattered enormously. Unstructured time in the space — people bumping into each other, unexpected encounters — was where the community grew its own ideas. A core group of deeply committed people formed organically, and Jesse found that the most important design challenge was creating a mechanism for people on the fringes to make their way inward, from casual visitors to genuine participants.</p>
