# Nuns, Nones, and Sacred Hospitality

> A gathering called "Nuns and Nones" brought together Catholic Sisters and unaffiliated millennials. On a Brooklyn rooftop, a nun blessed a community ritualist with oil, poetry, and liturgy — one of the most significant spiritual moments of his life. The takeaway: ritual works faster than conversation alone.

<p>Casper ter Kuile had spent years studying how people — especially younger people — were leaving institutional religion but still hungering for spiritual community. As co-founder of the Sacred Design Lab, he was exploring what 21st-century spiritual well-being could look like when it wasn't tethered to a denomination. The question kept circling back to something ancient: ritual and hospitality.</p>

<p>The breakthrough came through an unlikely encounter. A gathering called "Nuns and Nones" brought together Catholic Sisters and unaffiliated millennial community leaders to explore what tradition might mean in an age of disaffiliation. What happened wasn't debate or negotiation — it was transmission. The Sisters shared practices, stories, and ways of being that the younger participants had been trying to invent from scratch. The millennial leaders brought fresh energy and new questions. Both groups recognized something in the other.</p>

<p>One moment crystallized it. Sister Mary Dacey — a woman of deep theological grounding and integrity — blessed Casper with a religious name on a Brooklyn rooftop, using oil, poetry, and liturgy. It was personal, spontaneous, and deeply rooted in tradition. Casper describes the experience as one of the most significant spiritual moments of his life — and it happened outside any church, on a roof, between people who might never have met through conventional channels.</p>

<p>This encounter shaped Casper's conviction that ritual works faster than conversation alone. Shared rituals — even simple ones — create a felt sense of belonging that no amount of discussion can replicate. They mark time, create rhythm, and bind people to each other and to a place.</p>

<p>He put this into practice with Solstice in the Park, a public gathering in Brooklyn that brings neighbors together for workshops on meditation, embroidery, bird symbolism, and seasonal reflection. The event partners with the Brooklyn Public Library and draws immigrant educators alongside lifelong residents. It's not a religious service. It's a communal marking of time — an acknowledgment that the seasons turn and that we turn together.</p>

<p>Casper's thinking about community centers on three practices that function as relational technology. The first is ritual — structured, repeated actions that create belonging faster and deeper than informal socializing. The second is hospitality — and not just the passive kind. Casper talks about making people feel explicitly invited and wanted, countering the assumption that an open door is enough. The third is covenant — a dynamic, relational commitment that is sustained by love rather than enforced by rules.</p>

<p>These ideas found a home in The Dinner Party, co-founded by Lennon Flowers, which gathers small groups of people around shared meals and shared losses. What started as a space for grief became a model for how intimate gatherings — organized around vulnerability rather than networking — can form deep bonds.</p>

<p>Casper's work suggests that the most powerful relational technologies may be the oldest ones: a blessing, a shared meal, a seasonal gathering, a commitment to show up again. The innovation isn't in the form but in the willingness to practice these things in places and with people that institutions have stopped reaching. On a Brooklyn rooftop, between a nun and a none, a tradition found a new carrier — and a community found its rhythm.</p>
