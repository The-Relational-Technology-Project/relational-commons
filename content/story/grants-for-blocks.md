# Savannah's Grants for Blocks

> A city hall practiced ABCD with $500 at a time: resident groups on a block apply for micro-grants for self-chosen projects, judged by a resident committee with names redacted. The projects mattered less than the collaboration they required.

In 1993, Savannah's assistant city manager Henry Moore did something unusual with a $20,000 Ford Foundation grant: he asked residents what to do with it. The answer became **Grants for Blocks**: grants of up to $500 to groups of neighbors on a block, for improvement projects they chose and carried out themselves. Applications grew from 89 in the first year to more than 300; the city moved the program onto its own budget at $70,000 a year.

The governance details are the remixable part. A **resident review committee** — two representatives per applying neighborhood — scored applications with names, dollar amounts, and neighborhood identifiers redacted; members couldn't review their own neighborhood's bids. Early rounds skewed toward beautification, so the program learned to prefer projects that ran as ongoing community activity rather than one-day cleanups. The city's own administrator named the real product: 'the real benefit is not just in the projects themselves, it is in the collaboration they inspire.'

A block that applies for a $500 grant has to do everything this shelf teaches at miniature scale: find its people, agree on what it cares about, inventory who can do what, commit to each other, and finish something visible together. Moore went on to co-write the standard manual on combining ABCD with organizing — Savannah was his proof.

What it teaches: small money, seriously governed, is leadership development infrastructure. The grant is the excuse; the block meeting is the point.
