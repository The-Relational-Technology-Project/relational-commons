# The Kindness Party

<p>In Brownsville, Brooklyn — home to about 100,000 people and the largest concentration of public housing in North America — Teju Ravilochan founded GatherFor on a deceptively simple belief: in community, we have everything we need.</p>

<p>GatherFor organizes neighbors into two kinds of teams. Neighbor teams are groups of five who form a kind of chosen family. They meet regularly, share stories, and become friends. Over time, they wind up watching each other's kids, pooling funds to pay bills, visiting each other in the hospital, helping each other find jobs. GatherFor supports them with $300 a month for in-person gatherings and a matched savings practice called Susu — a Caribbean tradition where everyone pools money and gives the whole amount to one person on a rotating basis.</p>

<p>Then there are action teams — groups focused on a shared neighborhood challenge like housing, mental health, childcare, or entrepreneurship, each with a $5,000 budget.</p>

<p>But the story that best captures GatherFor's approach is the Kindness Party.</p>

<p>Teju was invited to speak at a tenant association meeting for Glenmore Plaza, one of the big housing projects. The room was full of angry residents. One woman told a story about a grab bar in her bathroom that had rotted so badly it came out of the wall — she fell, hit her head, and needed brain surgery. Others nodded. Repairs hadn't been made in years.</p>

<p>When it was Teju's turn, he set aside his prepared remarks and just asked questions. Had they tried speaking to the housing authority with a single voice? They hadn't. Trust was low. People were afraid.</p>

<p>A mentor told Teju that a great way to build trust is to help people with their actual issues. So he went back and proposed they organize together to hold NYCHA accountable. Most people were skeptical. But eight residents — out of fifty — were willing to try.</p>

<p>They went door-to-door collecting repair requests and documented everything in a big spreadsheet. Then came the creative turn. Inspired by a friend who had organized a block party to bridge a conflict, Teju proposed: instead of yelling at NYCHA, what if they celebrated the agency for doing the right thing? They brought NYCHA the spreadsheet and said: make these repairs within three months, and we'll throw a block party in your honor, with press and community. We'll celebrate you.</p>

<p>NYCHA accepted. They made 1,600 repairs in 60 days — 87% of what residents had requested. Since then, they've made over 5,000 repairs. Teju calls it "accountability through celebration" — not making anyone the villain, but offering a deadline, a team, and recognition. The carrot, the stick, and the teamwork together produced something none of them could alone.</p>

<p>What Teju wants people to understand is that this work isn't extraordinary. It comes down to capacities we all have: being present, listening, sharing stories, asking questions. In community, we have everything we need.</p>
