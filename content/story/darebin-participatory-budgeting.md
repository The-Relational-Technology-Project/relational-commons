# Darebin Participatory Budgeting

> A working-class Melbourne suburb gave 44 randomly selected residents actual power over two million dollars in infrastructure spending. Every recommendation was adopted unanimously — and the deeper outcome was a shift in who gets to decide.

<p>Darebin is a municipality in Melbourne's northern suburbs — diverse, working-class, and not the kind of place that usually makes headlines for democratic innovation. In 2014, the city council had two million dollars in infrastructure funding and a question: who should decide how to spend it? Rather than the usual process — council staff drafting proposals, a few residents showing up to public comment — they convened a citizens' jury. Forty-four residents were randomly selected from a pool of 3,000 invitation letters sent to households across the municipality, then stratified to ensure the jury reflected Darebin's actual demographics. The jurors were asked a single question: how should we best spend two million dollars to improve our community?</p><p>The jury met four times over four months. They heard from community organizations, reviewed project proposals, debated priorities, and voted. Their eight recommendations included $865,000 for a new neighbourhood house in Preston East — an area that had long lacked a dedicated community gathering space — along with funding for exercise equipment in public parks and multipurpose sports courts. Every single recommendation was adopted unanimously by the council. Not as suggestions to consider. Not as input to weigh alongside other factors. As decisions. The council treated the jury's recommendations as binding, a level of commitment that remains rare in participatory budgeting anywhere in the world.</p><p>The newDemocracy Foundation and the City of Darebin both commissioned independent research on what the process did to participants. The findings pointed to something beyond the budget outcomes: jurors reported increased trust in local government and a deeper sense of civic agency. People who had never attended a council meeting — who had assumed government happened elsewhere, to other people — discovered that they were capable of making consequential decisions about their own community. The two million dollars mattered. But the shift in who got to decide may have mattered more. Darebin's experiment suggests that participatory budgeting works best not when it asks citizens for their opinions, but when it gives them actual power.</p>
