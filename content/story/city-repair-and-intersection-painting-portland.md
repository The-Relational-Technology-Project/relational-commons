# City Repair and Intersection Painting, Portland

> In the 1990s, architect Mark Lakeman returned from Central America and led neighbors in painting a Portland intersection — sparking a movement of over 1,200 placemaking projects.

<p>In the mid-1990s, architect Mark Lakeman returned from villages in Central America where residents gathered in common spaces at the center of their communities. He looked at his own neighborhood in Portland, Oregon — the Sellwood neighborhood — and saw something different: intersections dominated by cars, where no one lingered. In 1996, neighbors at the intersection of 8th and Sherrett created a tea stand, a children's playhouse, and a community library on the corner. They renamed it Share-It Square. Then they decided to paint the intersection itself — a giant, colorful mural on the asphalt.</p><p>The Portland Department of Transportation was not amused. One city official told Lakeman: "That's public space. Nobody can use it." The neighbors went guerrilla. They painted without permission. The resulting confrontation eventually led, in January 2000, to Portland City Council passing an Intersection Repair ordinance — allowing neighborhoods to develop public gathering places in certain street intersections. The movement Lakeman founded, City Repair, has since been responsible for over 1,200 placemaking projects in Portland and beyond.</p><p>The Village Building Convergence — an annual ten-day event — brings together workshops on natural building, permaculture, and intersection repair, producing painted intersections, cob benches, information kiosks, and community gardens. Research has confirmed what neighbors already knew: people living near Intersection Repairs report being healthier, happier, and more content with their neighborhoods. Police calls decrease. The intersection — the most mundane piece of civic infrastructure — becomes a commons. The relational technology is the act of painting together. Neighbors of different ages and backgrounds gather with brushes and paint, co-creating something visible and permanent on the ground they share. The mural marks the place. But the painting builds the community.</p>
