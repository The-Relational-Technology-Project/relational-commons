# France's Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat

> After the Yellow Vest protests exposed a crisis of democratic legitimacy around climate policy, France convened 150 randomly selected citizens who produced 149 concrete policy measures — proving ordinary people can craft sophisticated climate policy, and revealing what happens when government treats deliberation as co…

<p>In the autumn of 2018, the Gilets Jaunes — Yellow Vests — erupted across France. Hundreds of thousands of people, many from rural and peri-urban areas, blocked roundabouts and marched against a fuel tax that President Macron had framed as essential for climate action. The protests exposed a crisis of democratic legitimacy: the people most affected by climate policy had no role in shaping it. Macron's response was unprecedented in French history. He launched a Grand Débat National, and from it emerged a proposal to convene 150 randomly selected citizens — stratified by age, gender, education, geography, and urban-rural balance — to answer a single question: how can France reduce its greenhouse gas emissions by at least 40 percent by 2030, in a spirit of social justice?</p><p>The Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat met over seven weekends between October 2019 and June 2020, surviving even the disruption of COVID-19 by shifting some sessions online. Members heard from climate scientists, economists, industry representatives, and activists. They worked in thematic groups on housing, transport, food, production, and consumption. By the end, they had produced a 460-page report containing 149 concrete policy measures — from banning domestic flights where train alternatives exist, to retrofitting buildings, to enshrining climate protection in the French constitution. Macron initially embraced the work, committing at the Élysée Palace to support 146 of the 149 measures. Then the watering-down began. The climate law that eventually passed in 2021 implemented only a fraction of the convention's ambition.</p><p>The aftermath was messy and instructive. Some convention members formed an advocacy group called "Les 150" to push for full implementation. Macron, frustrated by their persistence, reportedly said: "They helped me at the start, now they want everything." The tension revealed something important about the relationship between deliberative and representative democracy: citizens who have genuinely deliberated don't simply hand over their recommendations and go home. They develop ownership, expertise, and a sense of democratic entitlement that outlasts the process itself. France's climate convention is both an inspiration — proof that 150 ordinary people can produce sophisticated, actionable climate policy — and a cautionary tale about what happens when the political system treats citizen deliberation as consultation rather than co-governance.</p>
