# 596 Acres: Signs on Fences

> A lawyer turned a city spreadsheet of vacant public land into maps — and zip-tied physical signs to lot fences saying who owned the land and how to organize for it. 143 lots became gardens, farms, and plazas.

Paula Z. Segal obtained a New York City spreadsheet listing all publicly owned vacant land in Brooklyn — 596 acres of it — and did two things with it. The expected one: put it online as a map (which grew into Living Lots NYC). And the brilliant one: **zip-tied laminated signs to the chain-link fences of the lots themselves**, telling whoever walked past exactly who owned this land, that it was public, and how to organize with neighbors to get access.

The map found the data; the signs found the people. The online tool added a crucial social feature — strangers who cared about the *same lot* could find each other — and 596 Acres supported 143 neighbor-led campaigns that turned NYC vacant lots into community gardens, farms, and play spaces before the organization sunset. The model spread: Grounded in Philly mapped 32,000+ vacant Philadelphia lots with the Public Interest Law Center; Chicago's Large Lots program (a sibling story on this shelf) turned the same insight into $1 city lot sales.

What it teaches: public data becomes neighborhood power through three moves — map it, **post it physically at the exact place it describes**, and wire it so people organizing the same place meet each other. The fence sign is the most underrated interface in civic tech.
