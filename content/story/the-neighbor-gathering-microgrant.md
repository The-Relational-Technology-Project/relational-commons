# The Neighbor Gathering Microgrant

> With $3,000 and three volunteers, Sam Pressler launched a microgrant pilot in Charlottesville giving $100 to 30 hosts across 25 neighborhoods. They expected five applications and got 70. The framework: permission to overcome inertia, accountability from the money, and a commitment to do it again.

<p>It started as a refrain. Every time someone asked Sam Pressler, "What's one thing any community could do right now to strengthen local civic life?" he'd say some version of: every community should offer microgrants for neighbors to gather their neighbors. Block parties, barbecues, neighborhood olympics — whatever. The important thing is to get neighbors out interacting.</p>

<p>He said it so many times he became convinced of it himself. So in early 2024, on a bit of a whim, he launched a microgrant pilot in Charlottesville, Virginia. With $3,000 from a fellowship at UVA's Karsh Institute of Democracy and three volunteers, they built the entire initiative from scratch: a simple application (3-5 minutes to fill out), same-day funding approval, a toolkit with a checklist and facilitation guide, and a lightweight reporting form.</p>

<p>They distributed $100 microgrants to 30 different hosts representing 25 neighborhoods across the area. Those hosts brought together roughly 500 neighbors. A local resident had predicted they'd be lucky to get five hosts. They got 70 applications.</p>

<p>The variety was one of the biggest surprises. About half hosted shared meals. Others organized block parties, seed-starting events in the spirit of spring, gatherings for parents of young children, and — memorably — one beer tasting for 20+ people whose host reported three key takeaways: people like tasting beers, people like sharing food, people like doing both together.</p>

<p>The framework that emerged was simple but powerful: permission, accountability, commitment. The official microgrant gave hosts permission — an excuse to overcome inertia or shyness or post-pandemic withdrawal. The $100 created accountability — once you've received money, you can't easily back out. And the facilitation guide prompted hosts to close each gathering with a collective commitment, shifting the orientation from one-off event to beginning of something ongoing.</p>

<p>The commitments were specific. Neighbors pledged to host recurring potlucks, annual block parties, Shabbat dinners, and seasonal gatherings. Some committed to building a music stage for a neighborhood market, painting murals, or petitioning for speed bumps. Others started shared newsletters, photo albums, and neighborhood wikis. Nearly all of them followed through.</p>

<p>The pilot's most striking quality was its simplicity. No logic model. No five-year strategic plan. No rigorous reporting. Just a small amount of money, a basic toolkit, a few reminder emails, and trust that neighbors know how to bring their neighbors together.</p>

<p>The initiative has since expanded through a partnership with the Relational Tech Project, relaunching at withneighbors.org with $50 microgrants available nationally. The application is still simple: who you are, where you live, your best guess for a gathering date, and your proposed vision. The long-term hope is that local community foundations, nonprofits, and government offices will adopt and remix the model in their own places. If a self-described "goof" could make it work in a few months with $3,000, anyone can.</p>
