# vTaiwan and Digital Democracy

> Taiwan's Sunflower Movement led to vTaiwan — a digital deliberation platform using Polis that surfaced consensus among 200,000 users on 28 issues.

<p>In 2014, Taiwan's Sunflower Movement — a wave of protests over controversial trade legislation with China — mobilized civil society in unprecedented ways. Among those who responded was g0v, a civic technology community founded in 2012 to promote digital transparency. Government minister Jaclyn Tsai attended a g0v hackathon and was so impressed that she invited the community to design a neutral platform for large-scale public deliberation. The result was vTaiwan.</p><p>At its heart was Polis — a tool developed by a Seattle-based team — which gathers, analyzes, and visually maps what large groups of people think, in real time. The design was deliberately counter to social media dynamics: users could agree or disagree with statements, or write their own, but they could not reply to each other. There were no threads, no arguments, no pile-ons. Instead, the system surfaced areas of consensus — the things people across different opinion clusters actually agreed on. Audrey Tang, a retired Silicon Valley entrepreneur and g0v cofounder, became the bridge between civic tech and government. Appointed as Minister without Portfolio in 2016 at age thirty-six — the youngest minister in Taiwan's history and the first non-binary person in cabinet — Tang championed vTaiwan as a new form of democratic infrastructure.</p><p>A defining success was Uber regulation. Initially, opinions were deeply polarized between taxi industry defenders and ride-sharing advocates. But as users revised their positions, consensus emerged around a level playing field. The government created new regulations informed by the consensus found on vTaiwan. At its peak, vTaiwan had roughly 200,000 users — about one percent of Taiwan's population — and held deliberations on 28 issues, 80 percent of which led to legislative action. The platform demonstrated that digital tools, designed with care for relational dynamics rather than engagement metrics, can help large groups of strangers find common ground. Taiwan's experiment suggests that the most important design choice in civic technology is what you don't build — no reply buttons, no virality, no outrage loops — as much as what you do.</p>
