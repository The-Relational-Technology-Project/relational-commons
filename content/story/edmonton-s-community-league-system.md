# Edmonton's Community League System

> Since 1917, Edmonton has built a system of 157 community leagues — independent neighborhood nonprofits covering the entire city with halls, gardens, and civic infrastructure.

<p>In 1917, residents of a neighborhood in Edmonton, Alberta, formed the city's first community league. By 1921, nine leagues existed, and the Edmonton Federation of Community Leagues was established to support them. Today, virtually every residential community in Edmonton has a corresponding community league — 157 in all. Each league is an independent nonprofit volunteer organization with its own bylaws, policies, and governing structure. They advocate to the municipal government on behalf of their community, publish newsletters, and — critically — provide shared amenities: community halls, tool libraries, sports facilities, community gardens.</p><p>Edmonton's community leagues represent one of the most complete examples of neighborhood-level civic infrastructure in North America. What makes the system remarkable is its combination of universality and independence — the leagues cover the entire city, but each one is distinct, self-governed, and responsive to the particular character of its place. In recent years, Edmonton has layered an explicit Asset-Based Community Development practice onto this infrastructure through the Abundant Community Edmonton initiative. Neighborhood Connectors and Block Connectors help residents identify strengths — skills, passions, local knowledge — and build from them rather than from deficits. The city has shifted its own role, too: Community Resource Coordinators serve as touchpoints for neighborhoods, supporting community-led work rather than directing it.</p><p>Edmonton's lesson for relational technologists is about the long arc. A community league system that began over a century ago is still functioning, still evolving, and still providing the civic soil in which neighbor-to-neighbor connection can take root. The technology is governance structure — bylaws, elected boards, community halls. The innovation is that it has been sustained, generation after generation, by volunteers who believe their neighborhood matters.</p>
