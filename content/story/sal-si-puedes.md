# Sal Si Puedes: The House Meeting That Found Cesar Chavez

> In 1952, organizer Fred Ross came to a San Jose barrio called 'Get Out If You Can.' A 25-year-old named Cesar Chavez arranged to have the house meeting in his own home interrupted so he'd have an excuse to leave. He stayed.

Fred Ross Sr. spent the late 1940s organizing Mexican-American communities across Southern California for the Community Service Organization, refining a deceptively simple instrument: the **house meeting** — a small gathering in someone's living room, hosted by a resident who invites their own people. Cycles of house meetings built toward a big organizing meeting, then a founding convention. The living room did two things a rally never could: it let an organizer talk with people inside real trust networks, and it revealed leaders — you watched who convened people, who spoke with authority in their own kitchen. The CSO's proof of power came fast: roughly a thousand members and 15,000 newly registered voters in East L.A., and in 1949 Edward Roybal became the first Mexican-American on the Los Angeles City Council since the nineteenth century.

In spring 1952, Ross arrived in Sal Si Puedes — 'get out if you can' — a barrio of East San Jose, and a public-health nurse pointed him to Cesar Chavez, a 25-year-old Navy veteran. Chavez, assuming Ross was another patronizing outsider, arranged for the June 9 house meeting in his own home to be interrupted so he'd have an exit. Instead he was captivated — by an approach built on listening to people, engaging what they actually cared about, and moving them to act together. Ross wrote in his journal that night: *'I think I've found the guy I've been looking for.'*

Chavez became a CSO organizer; Ross soon recruited Dolores Huerta in Stockton, whose CSO lobbying won pensions for some fifty thousand non-citizen farmworkers. The lineage is literal: Ross trained Chavez and Huerta; the union they built trained Marshall Ganz; Ganz's framework trained a generation. One living room at a time.
