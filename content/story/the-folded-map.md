# The Folded Map

> Chicago's North and South Sides have mirrored street addresses but vastly different realities. Artist Tonika Lewis Johnson photographs corresponding addresses side by side, then introduces the people living at those "map twin" locations to each other — turning abstract inequality into something personal and proximate.

<p>Chicago's North and South Sides have corresponding street addresses — the same numbers, mirrored across the city's dividing line. But the neighborhoods at those matching addresses often couldn't be more different. Decades of segregation, disinvestment, and policy created two Chicagos living side by side on paper but worlds apart in reality.</p>

<p>Tonika Lewis Johnson, a visual artist from the South Side's Englewood neighborhood, started the Folded Map Project to make this visible — and then to do something about it. The project begins with photography: Tonika captures side-by-side images of corresponding addresses on the North and South Sides. A house at 6700 North next to its mirror at 6700 South. The visual contrast tells a story no policy paper can: the same city, vastly unequal.</p>

<p>But the project doesn't stop at images. Tonika introduces the people who live at these mirrored addresses — "map twins" — to each other. They meet, visit each other's neighborhoods, share meals, and see each other's daily reality up close. The relationships that form aren't symbolic. They're real encounters between real people who happen to share a number but not a lived experience.</p>

<p>The Folded Map Project has been embraced by the City of Chicago, which partnered with Tonika to distribute action kits. The project has been featured at galleries, community spaces, and public events, but its core engine is intimate: two people, two neighborhoods, one conversation at a time.</p>

<p>What makes Folded Map a piece of relational technology is that the photographs aren't the point — the relationships are. The images create an occasion for encounter. They give people a reason to cross boundaries they'd otherwise never cross. They turn abstract inequality into something personal and proximate.</p>

<p>For relational technologists, the Folded Map offers a pattern: use what a place already has (its geography, its structure, its hidden symmetries) to create connection. Don't build from scratch — fold the map, and see who's been standing across from you all along.</p>
