# The Five Sisters Forum: Slow by Design

> Front Porch Forum began as one Burlington neighborhood's hand-compiled nightly email digest. Twenty-five years on, half of Vermont's adults are members — and the studied effects are offline: more volunteering, more neighbors talking.

In 2000, Michael and Valerie Wood-Lewis — new to Burlington, Vermont's Five Sisters neighborhood and wanting to meet neighbors — started an email list advertised with paper flyers slipped door to door. For six years Michael hand-compiled each day's five or six messages into one nightly digest. In 2006 the practice became Front Porch Forum, now covering every Vermont town.

The constitution barely changed: members verified to a real address; one forum per place; **one digest email a day**; a dozen paid human moderators; no algorithm, no comments, no infinite scroll. It's a Vermont public benefit corporation funded by local ads — and it deliberately refused to scale beyond one state, the exact trade the graveyard's dead hyperlocal networks refused to make.

The evidence is the strongest in neighborhood tech: with 250,000 active members among Vermont's roughly 270,000 households — a majority of the state — a 13,000-user study by New_ Public and UT-Austin's Center for Media Engagement found 81% felt FPF made them more informed citizens (versus 26% for Facebook users and 32% for Nextdoor), and FPF members were significantly more likely to attend local events, talk with neighbors, and volunteer. When Tropical Storm Irene hit in 2011, arriving federal workers found towns already self-organized through their forums.

What it teaches: the code was never the product — the *pace* was. You can't fork the software (it's proprietary), but any block can fork the constitution: real names, one geography, one send a day, a human moderator. That's buildable in an afternoon.
