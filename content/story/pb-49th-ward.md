# The 49th Ward: America's First Participatory Budget

> In 2009, a Chicago alderman handed his entire $1.3M infrastructure budget to residents: assemblies, resident committees, 36 proposals, 1,652 voters, 14 projects built. The ritual mattered more than the tech.

In November 2009, Alderman Joe Moore of Chicago's 49th Ward (Rogers Park) did something no US elected official had done: he turned his entire $1.3 million annual 'menu money' infrastructure budget over to his constituents, working with the Participatory Budgeting Project to adapt the process born in Porto Alegre, Brazil.

The process was the technology: neighborhood assemblies where anyone could propose; six resident committees that spent the winter developing 36 proposals with cost estimates; then a public vote in April 2010 — 1,652 residents — funding fourteen projects: sidewalk repairs, bike lanes, community gardens, underpass murals, street lighting, dog-friendly park areas. The digital layer stayed thin, and the documented weakness was relational, not technical: outreach missed much of the ward's Latino population, the same inclusion lesson E-Democracy learned two states away.

PB then scaled: New York City council districts have run it since 2012 — over $210 million allocated across 706 projects — with the free, open-source Stanford PB Platform (a tool on this shelf) providing multilingual digital ballots. The Participatory Budgeting Project publishes free toolkits, and People Powered maintains a global hub and atlas.

What it teaches: PB is a repeatable neighborhood-scale democratic ritual with free tooling at every step — and its failure mode is always outreach, never software. Budget for door-knocking before ballots.
