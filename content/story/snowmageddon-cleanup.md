# Snowmageddon Cleanup: One Neighbor, One Weekend, One Map

> During DC's 2010 blizzards, one Dupont Circle resident stood up an Ushahidi map to coordinate digging out. The Washington Post pointed the city at it — and the next winter, other cities launched their own in under an hour.

When back-to-back blizzards dropped nearly three feet of snow on Washington DC in February 2010 and shut the federal government, Ryan Ozimek — a Dupont Circle resident who ran a nonprofit-sector web firm — stood up an Ushahidi deployment to coordinate his neighborhood's dig-out. 'I really thought it was going to be just a fun experiment with my friends.'

Within a day the Washington Post had adopted and promoted it as 'Snowmageddon Cleanup.' Ozimek watched the arc every mutual aid organizer knows: the first twenty-four hours were all requests — stuck cars, unplowed streets, elderly neighbors snowed in — and then neighbors began self-organizing to answer them, claiming requests the way Bed-Stuy Strong volunteers would claim Airtable tickets a decade later.

The remix proved the pattern: during December 2010's storm, versions launched for New York, Boston, Newark, and Point Pleasant Beach, New Jersey — a new city could stand one up **in under an hour**.

What it teaches: the purest documented case of this shelf's thesis. Global open-source infrastructure (built for Kenyan election monitoring), deployed by one neighbor for one neighborhood in a weekend, made real by a trusted local channel pointing people at it. The map did the matching; the shovels were already on the block.
