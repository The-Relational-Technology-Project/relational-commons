# The Grape Boycott: Farmworkers in Every City

> When the strike alone couldn't win, the United Farm Workers sent farmworkers — many of whom had never left California's Central Valley — to run boycott houses in distant cities. Seventeen million Americans stopped buying grapes.

On September 8, 1965, Filipino-American grape workers led by Larry Itliong's generation walked out at Delano, California; Cesar Chavez's largely Latino union voted to join them eight days later. The growers could outlast a strike in the fields. So the union moved the battlefield: it sent farmworkers themselves to cities across North America to run boycott houses — organizing local committees of students, clergy, unionists, and housewives to get California grapes out of the stores.

Eliseo Medina was sent to run the Chicago boycott office in his early twenties. Jessica Govea, a 21-year-old farmworker from Bakersfield, led the boycott in Toronto and Montreal and persuaded Canadian grocery chains to drop California grapes. Dolores Huerta directed New York and sharpened the strategy: target the chains, not just the fruit. Marshall Ganz — who had left Harvard for Mississippi Freedom Summer — joined the union in 1965 and spent sixteen years learning and building this machine.

At the boycott's height an estimated seventeen million Americans stopped buying grapes. On July 29, 1970, twenty-six Delano growers signed the first real union contracts in California field agriculture, covering roughly ten thousand workers.

What it teaches: distributed organizing is what a poor constituency does when its only abundant resources are its people, their stories, and their courage. Every boycott house was a snowflake node — local leadership, real responsibility, accountable to the whole. The pattern reappeared four decades later in a presidential campaign, and it reappears any time a neighborhood realizes its people in other rooms are a resource, not a diaspora.
