# The Village Square, Tallahassee

> A clinical social worker in Tallahassee started a civic forum built on a simple premise: sharing a meal with someone you disagree with is itself a form of democratic infrastructure. Nearly two decades later, the Village Square has expanded to six American cities.

<p>In 2006, Liz Joyner — a clinical social worker trained in family systems theory — had been volunteering in Tallahassee politics long enough to reach a conclusion: the way Americans work out their disagreements in public is fundamentally broken. Not because people disagree, but because they've lost the spaces and relationships that make disagreement productive. So she started the Village Square, a nonpartisan civic forum built on a deceptively simple premise: that the act of sharing a meal with someone you disagree with is itself a form of democratic infrastructure.</p><p>The signature program is Dinner at the Square, where 170 Tallahassee residents sit down together — a conservative Catholic priest and a liberal lesbian theatre owner might share the same table — and dive into structured conversations on contentious topics. Over nearly two decades, the Village Square has grown to more than thirty programs a year, partnering with local organizations across Tallahassee. Their programming ranges from "Speed Date Your Local Leaders" to the annual Tallahassee Town Hall to the "Club of Honest Citizens." The organization has expanded to six cities across America, training local teams to build their own Village Squares with ongoing support and infrastructure. Joyner's insight — rooted in her clinical training — is that political polarization behaves like a family systems problem: it escalates when people stop being in relationship, and it de-escalates when they start again.</p><p>What makes the Village Square different from most bridging organizations is its insistence on locality. It is not a national media campaign or a viral social media initiative. It is people in Tallahassee, in Sacramento, in other specific American cities, choosing to eat together and talk about hard things in the place where they actually live. Joyner often says that people are really hard to hate close up. The Village Square is a bet that deliberative democracy doesn't begin with policy papers or assemblies — it begins with proximity, a shared table, and the civic courage to stay in the room when the conversation gets uncomfortable.</p>
