# E-Democracy: The Forums Built by Door-Knocking

> Minneapolis–St. Paul's neighborhood forums became genuinely inclusive the only way it happens: 129 in-person outreach events, organizers speaking ten languages, and three thousand paper sign-ups gathered on doorsteps.

Steven Clift's E-Democracy.org — a pioneer of online civic space since 1994 — launched neighborhood Issues Forums across Minneapolis–St. Paul starting in 2007, including in Cedar-Riverside, a neighborhood of about 7,500 residents where thousands of Somali and Oromo neighbors lived alongside Latino, Korean, and Vietnamese communities.

The forums' real innovation wasn't software — it was the admission that inclusive online space is built offline. With Ford and Knight Foundation support, organizers ran **129 in-person outreach events**, door-knocked over two summers with a St. Paul team speaking **ten languages**, and gathered **three thousand sign-ups on paper**. The rules-based forums (real names, civility nudges, careful moderation) then had a membership that actually looked like the neighborhood.

The organization eventually wound down — founder-dependence and funding drought, the classic nonprofit causes — and its forums are archived. But its inclusion playbook is the permanent artifact, and the graveyard lesson is generous rather than grim: two decades of civility-by-design knowledge, free for the taking.

What it teaches: this is the bridge between this shelf and the organizing one. The tool is the 10%; the door-knocking in ten languages is the 90%. Any neighborhood platform that launches without an offline recruitment plan has chosen its demographics already.
