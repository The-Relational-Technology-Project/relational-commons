# Neighborhood Teams: The Snowflake at National Scale

> The 2008 Obama campaign trained volunteers in public narrative at 'Camp Obama,' structured them into neighborhood teams with real responsibility, and proved the Ganz model could hold millions of people.

In 2007, Marshall Ganz and colleagues designed a volunteer architecture for the Obama campaign that ran directly on the practices of this shelf: multi-day 'Camp Obama' trainings where volunteers learned to tell a story of self, us, and now — then organized into **neighborhood leadership teams** with defined turf and real responsibility for voter contact.

The structure was the snowflake: a team coordinator at the center of interdependent roles — canvass, phone bank, volunteer, data — each leading others, rather than staff controlling a call list. Journalist Zack Exley, embedded that fall, counted 1,231 campaign-defined neighborhoods in Ohio alone, and named the operating principle: respect the volunteers by giving them real responsibility. By Ganz's account the campaign trained roughly three thousand organizers who built thousands of neighborhood teams engaging over a million and a half volunteers; the definitive study, 'Groundbreakers,' puts the volunteer total at 2.2 million.

For this shelf, the campaign matters less as politics than as proof at scale: the house meeting, the 1:1, public narrative, testing leaders before escalating them, teams with shared purpose and ground rules — the whole toolkit held together with millions of strangers, most of whom had never organized anything. The lineage was explicit: Ganz learned the craft in the UFW, which learned it from Fred Ross's living rooms.

The question it leaves for a neighborhood: if the model works with 2.2 million people who mostly never met, what could it do with forty households who see each other every day?
