# South Australia Citizens' Jury on Nuclear Waste

> When South Australia considered storing nuclear waste from other countries, the government convened the largest citizens' jury in Australian history — 350 randomly selected residents who deliberated for six days and surfaced the relational deficit between government and citizens that made the proposal untenable.

<p>In 2016, the government of South Australia faced a question most politicians would rather avoid: should the state accept and store nuclear waste from other countries? A Royal Commission had produced a 318-page report recommending it. But the government knew that no one would actually read the report, and that public comment would be dominated by polarized activists on both sides. So they tried something different. Working with the newDemocracy Foundation, they designed the largest citizens' jury ever attempted in Australia — randomly selecting everyday residents to do what parliaments struggle to do: deliberate honestly on a question with no easy answer.</p><p>The process unfolded in two stages. First, a jury of fifty randomly selected South Australians — drawn from 25,000 invitation letters sent to random households — spent weeks distilling the Royal Commission's dense report into a few pages of accessible summary that anyone could understand. Then a second jury of 350 randomly selected citizens convened for six days of deliberation in Adelaide. They heard from expert witnesses, asked questions, debated in small groups, and wrestled with the tradeoffs. In the end, two-thirds voted against pursuing nuclear waste storage, citing a lack of trust in government processes as their primary concern. The Premier accepted the verdict. The technical and economic arguments for storage may have been strong, but the jury had surfaced something the Royal Commission couldn't: the relational deficit between government and citizens that made the proposal untenable.</p><p>The newDemocracy Foundation was candid about what didn't work. At 350 people, the jury was far larger than the typical twelve to fifty participants. Factions formed more easily. The timeline felt compressed. Some jurors in the larger second group reported less satisfaction than those in the intimate first jury. But those honest reckonings became some of the most valuable contributions to the field. The foundation published detailed research notes on every friction point — and other practitioners around the world used them to design better processes. The South Australia jury proved that citizens, given time and good information, can grapple with enormously complex questions. It also proved that the design of deliberation matters as much as the question being deliberated — and that the willingness to say what went wrong is itself a form of democratic practice.</p>
