# Yard Parties and Sweat Equity

<p>Before prison, Gerrel Jones never thought of being beneficial to anybody. Raised in a hostile environment, he became a hedonist. When he arrived in prison with a life sentence, he attempted suicide. But a life sentence makes you contemplative. While studying psychology, he read Viktor Frankl's <em>Man's Search for Meaning</em> and a light came on: Frankl saved his life by investing in the people dying around him. That selflessness principle changed everything.</p>

<p>Gerrel started building cohorts in prison — little communities of discipleship that grew into larger communities called faith dorms. When he was released after 20 years, community-building was the only skill he had.</p>

<p>He bought a house in the Birmingham neighborhood with the highest number of homicides. Then he started doing what he'd learned inside: knocking on doors, hosting fish fries and yard parties, canvassing the neighborhood. His budget never exceeded $10,000 a year. But he was consistent. That consistency — from 2017 to 2022 — built Renew Birmingham.</p>

<p>The model works block by block. First, shrink the service area so you can actually cover it. Renew Birmingham serves about four neighborhoods, 8,000 to 12,000 people. Then aggressively bring access to people rather than waiting for them to show up. Canvass. Knock on doors. Phone bank.</p>

<p>The second piece is incentivizing neighbors to host. Renew will give someone $100 to invite 25 neighbors to their yard, then bring food, music, a tent, and case managers with them. The host becomes a neighborhood hero, and the yard becomes an access point for real services — housing, education, workforce development, health and wellness.</p>

<p>The third piece is sweat equity. Rather than just receiving services, neighbors barter volunteer effort at about $20 an hour. They earn credits they can use to pay a delinquent bill or get critical home repairs. The volunteering — canvassing, picking up trash, painting houses, attending school events — puts people in cohorts working toward a common goal. That's where accountability, responsibility, and community form.</p>

<p>When a drug dealer moved onto Gerrel's street, his elderly neighbor across the way raised the alarm. Gerrel went to the dealer's house, knocked on the door, introduced himself, and just talked. He didn't mention the dealing. He just let them know he was their neighbor. For a criminal, that kind of presence is uncomfortable — it disrupts the fear and intimidation they rely on. Ten days later, the dealers were gone.</p>

<p>Gerrel's theory is basic physics: if you mobilize the majority of people who want something good, the negative forces can't stay. You don't need a rebellion. You need positive spaces that crowd out the negative ones. As he puts it: compassion can't sit back and watch. Compassion is a participant in social change.</p>

<p>His advice to anyone interested in revitalizing a community: understand that it cannot be done from the outside. America has never seen a revitalization program that worked from the outside-in. You have to get your hands dirty, engage the affected population, and be consistent. It has to be done through you and the influence of your social capital — which is your time and attention.</p>
