## What It Is

A restorative justice process based on indigenous teachings that brings together those affected by harm—victims, offenders, families, and community members—in structured, intentional circle spaces. Rather than focusing on punishment, circles facilitate healing, accountability, and community reintegration.

## How It Works

Peacemaking circles create safe space for story-sharing and collective problem-solving: (1) Participants sit in circle formation, creating equal physical and symbolic space. (2) Inner and outer frames structure participation and ensure safety. (3) Facilitators ask all participants what harm has occurred, what healing is needed, and what repairs could support restoration. (4) Victims and offenders share their experiences; community members contribute wisdom and support. (5) The group collaboratively develops accountability and repair plans that address harm while reintegrating the offender. (6) Follow-up support ensures ongoing accountability and healing. The process is slower than conventional justice, intentionally creating space for understanding, emotional expression, and genuine healing.

## Who 'S Done It

Peacemaking circles have been implemented in criminal justice systems, schools, families, and communities worldwide. Indigenous communities have used circles for centuries. Contemporary applications in North America and Europe show reduced recidivism and increased healing outcomes compared to punitive approaches. Schools use circles for discipline; families use them for conflict resolution; communities use them for collective accountability.

## Connections

Emerges from indigenous wisdom traditions, particularly Haudenosaunee and other First Nations practices. Kay Pranis and other contemporary practitioners have adapted circles for modern conflict resolution. Relates to restorative justice, transformative justice, conflict resolution, and community accountability frameworks.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: Multiple sessions: pre-circles (2-4 weeks), main circle (2-4 hours), follow-up circles (ongoing)
- *People*: Facilitators with training; affected community members; 8-50+ participants
- *Space*: Quiet, safe space allowing circle formation; often community centers, schools, or council chambers
- *Materials*: Talking piece, optional: candles, plants, opening/closing readings
- *Skills*: Circle facilitation, trauma-informed practice, conflict resolution, community organizing
- *Energy Level*: Medium-to-high; requires sustained commitment and emotional presence
