## What It Is

A participatory, strengths-based process where community members together identify, document, and map the human, social, organizational, cultural, and physical resources already present in their neighborhood. Asset mapping shifts focus from problems and deficits to capacities, relationships, and existing strengths.

## How It Works

Community members gather to identify assets in their neighborhood—which might include individual skills and knowledge, community organizations and associations, institutions like schools and libraries, businesses, public spaces and infrastructure, and cultural traditions. Using paper maps, digital mapping tools, or online platforms, participants visually chart these assets geographically. The process involves walking the neighborhood, interviewing residents and leaders, and recording both obvious assets (parks, schools) and hidden assets (neighborhood elders' expertise, informal networks, community culture). The resulting map becomes a planning tool that helps communities see their own capacity, build on existing strengths, and identify where connections can be made between assets. Asset mapping is fundamentally about connection—linking neighbors to each other and to the resources and networks already present in their community.

## Who 'S Done It

Used by community development organizations, urban planners, public health departments, and grassroots neighborhood groups across North America. Philadelphia, Evanston, and many cities have integrated asset mapping into neighborhood planning. Community science organizations and participatory research initiatives use this practice.

## Connections

Rooted in Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD) framework developed by John Kretzmann and John McKnight. Connected to participatory planning, community research, and strengths-based approaches. Related to community visioning, participatory budgeting, and neighborhood planning processes.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: 6-12 weeks for comprehensive mapping; individual sessions: 2-3 hours
- *People*: 10-50+ community members; 1-2 facilitators/coordinators; optional: professional mappers or technicians
- *Space*: Community center, library, neighborhood meeting space; public spaces for walking tours
- *Materials*: Large paper maps or digital mapping platform (ArcGIS, Maptionnaire, or open-source tools); markers, photos, sticky notes; notebooks; optional: camera, GPS units, video
- *Skills*: Community facilitation, mapping (digital or paper), listening, data recording, relationship-building
- *Energy Level*: Medium—involves both gathering and collaborative analysis
