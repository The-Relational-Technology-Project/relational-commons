## What It Is

A small group of 3-6 people who commit to meeting regularly to provide each other with peer support, practical help, and emotional accountability. Microsolidarity formalizes the kind of deep mutual support that used to happen naturally in tight-knit communities but now requires intentional practice.

## How It Works

Find 3-4 people you want to know at a deeper level. Invite them to a time-bounded experiment rather than an open-ended commitment — 4-6 meetings over several months. This lowers the stakes. At each meeting, use the 'Case Clinic' technique: one person shares their real challenge or situation (10 min), the group asks clarifying questions (10 min), the group offers perspectives while the person listens without defending (15 min), then the person reflects on what was useful (10 min). Rotate so everyone gets focused attention. Keep meetings to about an hour. After your agreed-upon meetings, have a retrospective: Did this work? Do we want to continue? If yes, explore other activities — co-working sessions, retreats, skill-sharing. Build in low-pressure check-ins between meetings: a text, a voice note, a monthly coffee.

## Who 'S Done It

Richard Bartlett developed microsolidarity after years in cooperative and decentralized organizations. One documented crew of four people — two teachers, a nurse, and a freelancer — started meeting after one experienced burnout and isolation. They began with case clinics and quickly moved to practical mutual aid: meals when someone's parent got sick, brainstorming sessions for a work crisis, accountability for someone starting a business. By year two, they were co-working monthly and had become each other's primary support system. Several said it was as meaningful as therapy but more tailored to their actual lives.

## Connections

Microsolidarity is rooted in feminist consciousness-raising circles and popular education traditions. It draws on peer coaching methodologies from organizations like Enspiral. It connects to mutual aid pods, community accountability practices, and men's/women's circle traditions. It's designed for the modern condition of dispersed networks, offering a way to rebuild reciprocal support that used to happen in extended families and tight neighborhoods.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: 1-2 hours per meeting, monthly or biweekly, plus informal check-ins
- *People*: 3-6 people with baseline trust and willingness to be vulnerable
- *Space*: Quiet room (in-person or online) where people can speak freely
- *Materials*: Minimal — maybe a timer, notebooks
- *Skills*: Listening, asking good questions, comfort with vulnerability
- *Energy Level*: Moderate — emotionally demanding but not logistically complex
