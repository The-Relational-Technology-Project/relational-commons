## What It Is

Communal meal practices where people gather and each bring a dish to share, creating collective abundance and building community connection. While potlucks have deep roots in North American culture, similar practices exist internationally (potlach traditions, harvest feasts, communal meals), all centered on the principle that feeding each other is fundamental to community.

## How It Works

One person or group coordinates a potluck gathering, inviting community members to each contribute a dish (main, side, dessert, beverage, etc.) based on their means and abilities. Everyone eats together, sharing food prepared by multiple hands. The distribution of responsibility for feeding the group embodies core principles: collective action is more sustainable and empowering than reliance on a single provider; everyone's contribution is valued; people connect through the act of feeding each other. Potlucks can be organized formally (sign-up sheets indicating what's needed) or informally (everyone brings something). International and cultural variations include harvest festivals, communal cooking traditions, and multi-household feeding practices that emphasize reciprocal care. Modern potluck movements (like Potluck Nation and community organizing initiatives) use potlucks explicitly as tools for building social connection and collective power in neighborhoods and organizations.

## Who 'S Done It

Religious congregations, schools, workplaces, neighborhoods, cultural communities, and organizing groups across North America; Potluck Nation movement (launched to create 'movement culture'); millions of people gathering for neighborhood, organizational, and celebration-based potlucks. International examples: potlach traditions (Pacific Northwest Indigenous), harvest festivals worldwide, community meal traditions across cultures.

## Connections

Rooted in Indigenous potlatch traditions and communal food practices across cultures. Connected to communal meals, block parties, and food-sharing traditions. Part of broader gift economy and mutual aid movement. Related to community building, collective care, and de-commodified social connection. Connected to Food Not Bombs and other food justice movements.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: Planning: 2-4 weeks; event: 2-3 hours
- *People*: 1-2 organizers; 10-100+ participants
- *Space*: Community space (park, church, school, community center, neighborhood) that can accommodate gathering and eating
- *Materials*: Dishes and utensils, tables and seating, napkins/serving utensils, optional: signage, drinks, decorations
- *Skills*: Coordination, inclusive invitation, basic logistics, ability to affirm and welcome diverse contributions
- *Energy Level*: Low to medium—mostly celebratory and relational
