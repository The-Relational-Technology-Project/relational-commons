## What It Is

A neighborhood gathering that closes off a street and brings residents together for food, games, and conversation. Block parties build relationships between neighbors who might otherwise pass by each other daily, creating informal spaces where kids play freely and adults get to know one another without formal structure or cost.

## How It Works

Start by forming a small organizing committee of 3-5 people. Choose a date at least two months out — avoid holiday weekends. Check local city ordinances and get a permit (usually free or low-cost; this often comes with benefits like street barricades). Pick a location with enough open space for tables, activities, and movement. Create simple flyers and distribute door-to-door with details: date, time, location, your contact info, and what food you're planning. Keep food simple — potluck style with grilled burgers or hot dogs as the main dish. Plan activities that engage all ages: face painting and sidewalk chalk for kids, giant Jenga and scavenger hunts for everyone. The key is simplicity: no elaborate themes, no social media staging, no expensive decorations. Plan for 2-4 hours. Make it clear everyone is welcome with no pressure to contribute. The point is putting phones away and meeting neighbors.

## Who 'S Done It

Block Party USA, founded by Vanessa Elias, has catalyzed block parties across the country and is now featured as an official resource in the U.S. Surgeon General's 'Recipes for Connection' initiative. In Portland, a neighborhood organized an annual block party after discovering that residents who'd lived on the same street for years didn't know each other's names. By year three, the block party spawned a community phone tree, a babysitting co-op, and neighbors helping each other with home repairs. The momentum from one simple gathering led to an informal mutual aid network that strengthened the block's resilience during an ice storm.

## Connections

Block parties are rooted in working-class and immigrant neighborhood traditions of outdoor gathering — from street festivals in Italian neighborhoods to church parking lot celebrations. They connect to the broader community building movement of the 1960s-70s that emphasized reclaiming public space. Today they're part of a wider toolkit including neighborhood mapping, community dinners, and mutual aid networks. They're often a first step that makes deeper practices like Good Neighbor Agreements or microsolidarity crews feel natural.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: 2-4 months planning, 4 hours for the event
- *People*: 3-5 person organizing committee, aim for 20+ neighbors
- *Space*: Open street or cul-de-sac, enough for tables and movement
- *Materials*: Tables, chairs, grills, trash cans, cups, plates, basic decorations (optional)
- *Skills*: Basic project management, comfort knocking on doors
- *Energy Level*: Moderate — doable in spare time
