## What It Is

A cohousing model combining seniors seeking affordable housing and community, foster families needing support, and the children in their care. Elders mentor children, families support elders, and everyone benefits from being part of something larger. A response to interconnected crises — foster care instability, senior isolation, housing unaffordability — tackled through community.

## How It Works

Communities consist of affordable units around shared space. Roughly one-third for adoptive/kinship families (committing to three or more children from foster care), one-third for seniors (55+), plus community programming space. Families receive support services including case management, counseling, workshops. Seniors commit to volunteering at least eight hours weekly — mentoring, tutoring, meals, childcare. This isn't volunteer tourism; it's genuine reciprocal care. Children have consistent adults beyond parents. Seniors have purpose and daily contact with young people. Monthly community meetings, shared meals, and intergenerational events create natural connection. The design encourages daily interaction — walkable distances, shared outdoor space, visible activity.

## Who 'S Done It

Bridge Meadows opened in Beaverton, Oregon in 2011 with 36 units. Results are striking: 100% of children remained with the same caregiver and attended the same school (vs. an average of five moves nationally). Academic performance improved for 73% of youth; mental health issues reduced for 63%. Senior health improved noticeably. One senior described it: 'The elders are providing love and support to the families, the families are doing the same, and even the children are giving back to the elders.' Expanded to Portland and Redmond with similar outcomes.

## Connections

Builds on cohousing practice and intergenerational living traditions. Synthesizes trauma-informed care, community development, aging research, and foster care reform. Connected to babysitting co-ops and resilience hubs by showing how intentional design and relationship address interconnected problems. Demonstrates that housing is not just shelter but a tool for healing.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: Seniors 8+ hours/week; 3-5 years to develop
- *People*: 30-50 household units, committed staff for coordination
- *Space*: Land or buildings for development; shared community space
- *Materials*: Construction/renovation resources, activity supplies
- *Skills*: Real estate development, nonprofit management, trauma-informed practice
- *Energy Level*: Very high for developers; moderate for residents once established
