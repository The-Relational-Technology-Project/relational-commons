## What It Is

An Indigenous practice from First Nations peoples where participants sit in a circle and use a talking piece (feather, stick, shell) to ensure equal voice, non-hierarchical participation, and deep listening. Only the person holding the piece may speak; all others listen respectfully.

## How It Works

Prepare the space by arranging chairs in a circle with no head position. Place a talking piece in the center. If appropriate, facilitate a sacred opening — smudging, prayer, or silence to center the group. Explain the practice and its Indigenous origins. The facilitator speaks first, holding the piece and sharing from the heart. When finished, they pass it to the next person (typically to the right). That person speaks uninterrupted. Participants may pass if they choose. Listening is core: people listen deeply without planning their response, without interrupting, without judgment. After everyone has spoken, the circle closes with reflection or gratitude. The entire circle is sacred space from opening to closing.

## Who 'S Done It

Talking circles have been used 'since time immemorial' by Indigenous peoples for communication, conflict resolution, healing, and decision-making. First Nations elders have generously shared this practice, knowing its potential to transform how we listen. Contemporary use spans classrooms, healing circles, conflict resolution, and organizations. Educators report that circles shift dynamics: people who rarely speak find voice; dominant voices naturally regulate; the quality of listening deepens. In schools, circles have supported Indigenous students and built cross-cultural understanding.

## Connections

Rooted in millennia-old wisdom of Indigenous peoples, representing a fundamentally different approach to communication than Western hierarchical models. Honors principles of non-violence, respect, and the sacred. Adopted by peace-building organizations, restorative justice practitioners, schools, and communities. Contemporary forms include healing circles, peacemaking circles, and community building circles. When using this practice, it's important to honor its Indigenous origins and not appropriate it without acknowledgment.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: 1-2 hours for a circle
- *People*: 4-20+ participants, trained facilitator, optionally an Indigenous knowledge keeper
- *Space*: Any space allowing people to sit in a circle, ideally quiet
- *Materials*: Talking piece (feather, stick, shell, stone), optionally sacred medicines for smudging
- *Skills*: Facilitation, deep listening, emotional presence, knowledge of origins
- *Energy Level*: Low-to-medium — sitting, speaking, listening
