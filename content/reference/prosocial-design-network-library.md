# Prosocial Design Network: Digital Intervention Library

> The source collection for this shelf: evidence-rated design patterns that make online spaces more trustworthy, connected, and civil, each with what it is, when to use it, the studies behind it, and an honest confidence rating.

The Prosocial Design Network (a 501(c)(3) nonprofit) curates research-backed digital interventions — design changes shown, or hypothesized, to promote prosocial outcomes online. Every pattern on this shelf comes from their library, verbatim apart from formatting.

## Reading the evidence ratings

Each pattern carries PDN's evidence rating, given by their Library Team (social and data scientists) by consensus. Their definitions, verbatim:

- **Validated**: At least two studies conducted by different research teams each provide strong evidence of the design pattern's effectiveness.
- **Convincing**: Strong evidence from at least one study that the design pattern is effective.
- **Likely**: Persuasive evidence from at least one study that the design pattern is effective, although more testing would be needed to have strong confidence in its effectiveness.
- **Tentative**: Initial, promising evidence that a design pattern may be effective, although more testing would be needed to have confidence in its effectiveness.
- **Mixed**: In cases where there is evidence a design pattern is effective as well as persuasive evidence that it is ineffective or may even have negative effects.
- **Emergent**: Design solutions with only qualitative evidence.
- **Inference**: Design solutions that lack direct evidence. Yet analogous studies, expert opinions, or first principles, suggest that they might work.
- **Unlikely**: In cases where there is robust evidence that a design pattern has no effect, i.e., “a null”.

Source: https://www.prosocialdesign.org/how-our-ratings-work

## Civic signals

Patterns are grouped by the New_ Public civic signal they amplify (https://newpublic.org/signals): **Welcome** (invite participation, ensure safety, humanize others), **Connect** (cultivate belonging, build bridges between groups), and **Understand** (show reliable information, promote thoughtful conversation, build civic competence).

## Using these at neighborhood scale

Most of this research was done on large platforms and in simulated feeds. A block's message board or a neighborhood map is smaller and the people know each other, so treat a rating as evidence about the mechanism, not a promise about your place. Patterns that slow people down, explain decisions, or set norms up front tend to carry over well; ranking and scoring patterns matter less when there isn't much to rank. Pick the pattern for the problem your neighbors actually have, and say in the app where the idea came from.
