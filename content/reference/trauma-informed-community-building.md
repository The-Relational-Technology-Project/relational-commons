## What It Is

An approach to community development and engagement that recognizes the impact of trauma on individuals and communities, and intentionally designs practices, policies, and environments to mitigate re-traumatization and support healing. It acknowledges that many communities have experienced systemic and historical trauma and builds resilience and collective power in response.

## How It Works

Trauma-informed community building operates on four foundational principles: Do No Harm (being aware of trauma and avoiding re-traumatization), Acceptance (meeting people where they are), Community Empowerment (centering self-determination and resident leadership), and Reflective Process (continuously learning and adjusting). Practically, this means: building trust and transparency in all community interactions; creating physical and psychological safety; elevating community voices throughout planning and implementation; offering varied ways to participate (accommodating different comfort levels); providing cultural outlets for collective processing (art, movement, music); organizing community-building activities (gardening, exercise, art projects); training local leaders in trauma-informed practices; and building support networks where people can process recurring trauma. The work is collaborative, recognizes the full humanity of people affected by trauma, and understands healing as collective rather than just individual.

## Who 'S Done It

Community development organizations in traumatized neighborhoods (areas affected by violence, poverty, disinvestment); public health departments; housing authorities; grassroots organizations led by people with lived experience of trauma. Practiced particularly in communities recovering from police violence, community violence, historical displacement, and systemic oppression.

## Connections

Rooted in trauma psychology research and adapted for community work by grassroots organizers. Connected to healing justice, restorative justice, transformative justice, and community healing practices. Related to peer support models, mutual aid, and collective care. Influenced by Indigenous healing traditions and African diaspora healing practices.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: Ongoing; planning: 3-6 months; implementation: 1-3+ years or longer
- *People*: Community members with lived experience of trauma; trained facilitators/organizers; mental health practitioners (ideally from the community); diverse leadership that reflects community demographics
- *Space*: Community centers, parks, cultural spaces, homes; multiple accessible gathering spaces; creates welcoming, safe environments
- *Materials*: Art supplies, plants/gardening materials, music/sound equipment, comfortable seating, culturally relevant materials; optional: training curricula, peer support guides
- *Skills*: Trauma awareness, facilitation, cultural competency, listening, flexibility, systems thinking, community knowledge
- *Energy Level*: Medium to high—emotionally and physically demanding but nourishing
