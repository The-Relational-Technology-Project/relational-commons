# Story Sharing Potluck Supper

## What It Is

A structured community meal where people bring dishes and share personal stories prompted by meaningful questions. Story suppers recognize that breaking bread together while listening to each other's narratives rebuilds social fabric and creates deeper understanding across differences.

## How It Works

Form an organizing team and set a date. Decide on a theme (food memories, place-based stories, values, migration). Create an invitation telling attendees they'll bring one dish and prepare a 2-3 minute story responding to a particular prompt. The prompt should be 'both personal and nonpartisan,' encouraging people to go deeper than small talk. Examples: 'Share a food from your childhood and what it meant to you' or 'Tell us about a meal that brought someone unexpected into your life.' Arrange the meal in a warm space. Move through phases: welcomes and explanation, a first round of story-sharing (a few volunteers to warm up the group), the meal itself with continued sharing, then a dessert round where a storyteller might perform a prepared piece. After the meal, collect recipes and stories from attendees and compile them into a commemorative cookbook to send home.

## Who 'S Done It

Community Plate has facilitated story suppers across Maine communities, from Lewiston to Lubec, creating intergenerational and cross-cultural connection. NPR featured their work in a 2025 story about fighting loneliness through community suppers. At one coastal Maine supper, an elderly immigrant shared how her mother's recipe was her anchor when arriving in America. A teenager heard this and realized her grandmother made the same dish. After the supper, the grandmother began teaching the teenager her full family cooking tradition — something that hadn't happened in years. The supper didn't 'solve' isolation but created a moment of real seeing and a new intergenerational relationship.

## Connections

Story suppers are rooted in ancient traditions of communal eating and oral storytelling, and in modern community organizing practices that value narrative. They connect closely to circle processes, Civic Saturdays, and the broader practice of breaking bread as a social act. They're influenced by popular education methods that recognize storytelling as knowledge-making distinct from expert lectures. They pair well with Public Narrative and community lore practices.

## Key Ingredients

- *Time*: 4-6 weeks planning, 3 hours for the event
- *People*: 2-3 organizers, 15-30 attendees
- *Space*: Community center, home, library meeting room with warm atmosphere
- *Materials*: Tables, chairs, potluck dishes, tablecloths, flowers, notebooks for recipes/stories
- *Skills*: Storytelling facilitation, comfort creating psychological safety
- *Energy Level*: Moderate — the emotional work of creating safety is the most demanding part
