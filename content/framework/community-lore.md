# Community Lore

Every durable community has lore: the inside jokes, the shared stories, the names for things only the group uses, the legends about the early days. Lore is not decoration. It is one of the things that makes a community feel like a community rather than a meeting.

This framework draws on writing by **Mia Quagliarello** and others at the [grouphug](https://grouphug.substack.com/) Substack on the role of lore in healthy community.

## What lore is

Lore is the accumulated cultural material that's distinctive to your community. It can include:

- **Inside jokes.** The thing that's funny because we were all there.
- **Founding stories.** How this gathering started. What the first meeting was like. The legend of the first attendee who's now an institution.
- **Names for things.** "The big potluck." "The night we ran out of chairs." "Mike's chair." Group-specific shorthand.
- **Recurring characters.** The person who always brings the dog. The neighbor who's always early. The kid who's grown up at these gatherings.
- **Rituals.** A specific way you open meetings. A song you always sing. A toast that's traditional. A specific dish someone always makes.
- **Catchphrases.** Things people in the community say that wouldn't make sense outside it.
- **Symbols.** A flag, a logo, a t-shirt, a particular kind of name tag, a special chair.

Lore accumulates over time. You can't manufacture it on day one. You can create conditions for it to grow.

## Why lore matters

Three functions.

**It produces "we-feeling."** Lore is the difference between a group of strangers and a group with a shared identity. When you can refer to "remember the time" and people remember, you are not strangers anymore.

**It welcomes regulars while protecting against extraction.** Regular participants get to enjoy the lore as their own. They become carriers of it. The lore creates a soft boundary: visitors are welcome, but regulars are home.

**It documents what matters.** What a community remembers about itself is what it values. Lore preserves the moments that taught the community who it was. New members learn the values by learning the stories.

## How lore forms

Mostly by accident. A few patterns that help:

**Repeated gatherings produce more lore than one-offs.** The first time something happens, it's a thing that happened. The fifth time it happens, it's "what we do." The tenth time, it's tradition.

**Telling stories builds lore.** When a regular tells a new member about "the time we tried to..." they're transmitting lore. Create space at gatherings where these stories get told. A retrospective at the end of the year. An informal "tell the newbies about" segment. Stories that get told become canonical.

**Naming things builds lore.** The unnamed gathering is harder to remember. The Front Yard Friday. The Stoop Crew. The Tuesday Soup. Naming makes a practice into a thing the community can carry.

**Photos and artifacts help.** Albums of past gatherings, a notebook where people write notes, a flag for the block party. The artifacts become evidence and reminders of the lore.

**Welcoming the wild moments.** The chaotic, weird, unexpected things that happen become lore. The time it rained on the block party and everyone moved inside. The time a stranger showed up and turned out to be a long-lost neighbor. Lore loves accidents.

## How to seed lore intentionally

You can't fake lore, but you can make space for it.

1. **Make your gathering recurring.** Lore needs repetition.
2. **Use the same physical space when possible.** The space accumulates meaning.
3. **Mark the milestones.** Five gatherings, one year, fifty meetings. The anniversaries themselves become lore.
4. **Document lightly.** Photos, brief journal entries, a community Slack or text thread. Just enough record to remember.
5. **Tell stories at gatherings.** When natural moments come up, retell the history. New members learn; old members feel seen.
6. **Welcome the weird.** Don't sanitize the funny, awkward, surprising moments. They become the gold of the lore.

## When lore goes wrong

Two failure modes.

**Lore that excludes.** Inside jokes that go on too long, references that new people can never understand, lore that's used as a way of signaling who's in and who's out. The corrective: practice explaining the lore when new people are present. Brief unpacking turns lore from a barrier into an invitation.

**Lore that ossifies.** Lore that prevents change. "We've always done it this way" can be a strength or a constraint. The corrective: occasionally revisit which traditions still serve the community and which are dead weight. Some lore should die when its time comes.

## The simple test

Could a regular tell a new neighbor a few stories that capture what your community is like? If yes, you have lore. If not, you haven't been at it long enough yet, or you haven't been telling the stories.

The fix in both cases is the same: keep showing up; keep telling the stories.
