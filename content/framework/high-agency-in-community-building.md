# High Agency in Community Building

A common pattern in neighborhoods: people see a thing that should exist, agree someone should do it, and wait for that someone to show up. The someone never shows up. The thing never exists. Everyone is frustrated, then resigned.

High agency is the opposite move. *I see the thing. I am the someone. I will start.*

This isn't about being a hero. It's about a specific orientation that the strongest community builders share. The framework here draws on writing by **Casey Rosengren** and others in the "high-agency person" tradition, applied specifically to neighboring.

## What high agency looks like in neighboring

A few specific moves:

**Skip the permission ask.** Most things you'd want to do in your neighborhood don't actually need permission. A porch hang, a block walk, a potluck, a newsletter, a Buy Nothing group, a tool library, a community fridge: none of these require approval. Some require permits (street closures, large events on public land), but most of the actual community-building work needs only a host and a couple of friends.

**Send the first invitation.** The most powerful sentence in neighboring is *I'm hosting X, come.* Not *would you be interested in X*. Not *we should do X sometime*. The specific, scheduled invitation.

**Be willing to host an empty room.** The first time you do almost anything in your neighborhood, attendance will be lower than you hoped. Maybe two people. Maybe nobody. High agency means doing it anyway, noting what you learned, and trying again. The second time has more people. The fifth time has regulars.

**Start before you're ready.** The information you need to do something well comes mostly from doing it. Waiting until you feel ready is waiting forever. Start the practice with what you know; refine as you go.

**Own the small things.** When you notice a need (a path that's broken, a flyer that's misleading, a neighbor who's missing, a meeting that needs better facilitation), the high-agency move is to take it on. Not all of it. Just the next piece.

**Make it easy for others to join.** High agency is not solo work. It's starting in a way that makes participation easy: clear invitation, low barrier, specific role. The high-agency person doesn't carry the whole load. They make the load joinable.

## Where this is different from "leadership"

Traditional leadership models often assume:
- You have a title or position.
- You direct others.
- You have a vision and bring people to it.

High-agency neighboring inverts most of this:
- No title required.
- You start; others may or may not follow.
- You hold a question or curiosity; you don't impose a vision.

The recipes in this book mostly require high-agency hosts and almost never require formal leadership. A [block party](../reference/block-party.md) doesn't need a president. It needs someone who orders the snacks and sends the texts.

## Common failure modes

**Mistaking talking about it for doing it.** Conversations about hosting a thing, planning meetings about a thing, brainstorming the thing: all of this can substitute for actually doing the thing. High agency notices when planning has become a substitute for action.

**Burning out by carrying too much.** The high-agency person is also at risk of becoming the only person doing anything. The corrective is to design participation in from the start: roles, rotations, distributed hosting, multiple conveners.

**Mistaking activity for impact.** Hosting many events is not the same as building durable community. Sometimes the high-agency move is to slow down, attend other people's gatherings, listen, and adjust before doing more.

**Performing high agency.** The point is the practice, not the persona. Some "high-agency" online discourse has become performative. Notice when "high agency" has become a brand rather than a tool.

## The simplest test

If you've been thinking about hosting something for more than a week, send the invitation today.

If you've been thinking about it for more than a month, the question has changed: *what's stopping you?* Often the answer is fear of low attendance, fear of being weird, fear of being seen trying. Name that fear. Then send the invitation today.
