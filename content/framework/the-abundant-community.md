# The Abundant Community

A framework from **John McKnight** and **Peter Block**, articulated in their 2010 book *The Abundant Community*. The book argues that healthy neighborhoods are organized around the gifts, skills, and assets that already exist within them, not around the deficits that need to be filled by professionals and institutions.

This framework underlies much of the asset-based community development (ABCD) tradition. It is one of the most important reference points for any neighborhood organizer.

## The core argument

Most contemporary communities have outsourced the work of community to institutions. Health to the medical system. Safety to police. Education to schools. Aging to retirement homes. Food to grocery chains. Care to professionals.

The result is a "consumer community" where neighbors are clients rather than participants. We pay for services that used to be reciprocal among neighbors. The institutional alternatives are often genuinely useful, but they have not replaced the community functions they substituted for. They have hollowed out community while leaving the underlying needs unmet.

McKnight and Block argue for the *abundant community*: a neighborhood where the gifts and capacities of residents are recognized, mobilized, and connected, and where institutions are partners to the community rather than substitutes for it.

## Three core moves

The abundant community framework proposes three shifts.

### From deficits to gifts

Most community planning starts with what's wrong: the poverty rate, the crime rate, the health disparities. This framing produces "needy" communities that wait for outside intervention.

The asset-based move: start by mapping the gifts. Every person has skills, knowledge, relationships, and resources. Every neighborhood has institutions, associations, physical assets, and stories. The strongest community development surfaces and connects these.

This is not naive positivity. Real problems exist. The point is that solutions usually emerge from the assets, not from the deficits. A community that knows its own capacities can address its own problems.

### From clients to citizens

In a consumer model, residents are clients of services. In a citizen model, residents are participants in their own community. The shift involves practical changes: who decides, who hosts, who organizes, who is paid.

Block writes about this as the "citizen way" vs. the "consumer way." The consumer way is faster and more passive. The citizen way is slower and more demanding. The citizen way produces deeper community.

### From institutions to associations

Institutions (hospitals, schools, government agencies, large nonprofits) deliver services. They are good at certain kinds of work and bad at others. They cannot produce community; only people in relationship can.

Associations (block clubs, faith groups, neighborhood committees, mutual aid networks, [microsolidarity crews](../recipe/microsolidarity-crew.md), [microgrant gatherings](../recipe/microgrant-gathering.md)) are the form in which neighbors organize themselves. They have less capacity than institutions in many ways and more capacity in others. They produce belonging.

The abundant community treats institutions as partners and associations as the engine.

## The four properties of an abundant community

McKnight and Block identify several properties of healthy communities. A simplified version:

**Visible gifts.** People know each other's skills, talents, and offerings. The gifts are surfaced through conversations, gatherings, and intentional asset-mapping.

**Hospitable space.** Neighbors gather in shared physical spaces (homes, community centers, libraries, parks) regularly enough that strangers become familiars and familiars become friends.

**Productive associations.** Residents form small groups for support, problem-solving, and joy. The groups don't require permission from institutions; they emerge from neighbor relationships.

**Mutual generosity.** When someone has a need, neighbors respond. When someone has something to give, they give it. Generosity is normal, not exceptional.

## The hidden gifts in any neighborhood

Most asset-mapping exercises surface gifts most people didn't know were there. Common categories that turn up:

- **Skill gifts.** Cooking, carpentry, repair, gardening, music, teaching, listening, language fluency, financial literacy, child care, elder care.
- **Knowledge gifts.** History of the neighborhood, knowledge of available resources, technical expertise, professional knowledge.
- **Time gifts.** Retirees with time. Stay-at-home parents with time. Students with time. People between jobs with time.
- **Connection gifts.** People who know everyone. People who can introduce. People who hold the social map of the block.
- **Space gifts.** Backyards, basements, garages, porches, dining rooms that can host. Tools, equipment, vehicles that can be shared.

The same mapping that identifies these gifts produces relationships. The asset-mapping process is itself an act of community building.

## How to start

A simple practice rooted in the framework:

1. **Make a list of your own gifts.** Skills, knowledge, time you could share, things you'd be willing to host or lead. Most people, when asked, can list 15 to 30 things they could offer.
2. **Ask three neighbors what they're good at.** Frame it casually. *I'm curious, what are you actually good at that most people don't know?* The conversations themselves are valuable.
3. **Hold a gift-mapping gathering.** Five to ten neighbors. Each person shares two or three gifts they could offer the block. Look at the list together. Surprises will emerge.
4. **Connect one giver to one receiver.** Someone's gift matches someone's interest. Make the introduction.
5. **Repeat.** The practice compounds. After a year of gift-mapping conversations, your neighborhood has become a different place.

## Limits and cautions

A few honest qualifications.

**Asset-based approaches can become an excuse for state withdrawal.** "The community has assets" can be used as cover for cutting public investment. The framework should be paired with continued advocacy for public goods, not used to replace them.

**Some communities have been so depleted that asset-mapping alone won't restart them.** Long-term displacement, generational trauma, and active disinvestment require more than community organizing. Honest practitioners hold both: asset-based community building plus continued demand for systemic resources and justice.

**Gifts must be invited, not extracted.** The framework can be misused as a way of getting volunteer labor from people who need paid work. Reciprocity is the principle. Gifts circulate; they are not harvested.
